// module 10 two hundred services, twelve that matter

AWS as a map, not a catalog.

AWS feels unlearnable because people study it as an alphabet of product names. It isn't — it's five questions every deployment answers (where does code run, where do files live, where does data live, how does traffic arrive, how do parts talk), each with a managed answer. Learn the questions, and two hundred services collapse into a map with twelve landmarks.

This module builds the map, tours the twelve, assembles them into three honest reference architectures for a MERN app, then covers the two things everyone learns by incident instead: IAM and the bill. The cats are deploying their café app. Against all advice. 🐈

01

The mental map — five questions, five aisles

beginner → the frame that makes everything else attachable

Every app you've ever deployed answers the same five questions. Compute: where does my code run? Object storage: where do files/uploads/builds live? Data: where does structured state live? Networking/edge: how does traffic find me, get balanced, get cached? Messaging: how do parts talk without holding hands? AWS is those five aisles, plus two cross-cutting layers over everything: IAM (who may do what) and observability (what's happening).

The second half of the frame is the managed-ness slider: for each aisle you choose how much you operate yourself. Compute runs from EC2 (a VM you patch) → ECS/Fargate (containers, no hosts) → Lambda (functions, no anything). More managed = less ops and less control, usually more per-unit cost and less to go wrong. Nearly every AWS decision is this slider wearing a product name.

PROFESSOR WHISKERS EXPLAINSFeeding a cat, at every point on the managed-ness slider: EC2 is adopting the cat — total control, you handle vet visits at 3am. ECS/Fargate is a cattery — you specify the diet, they run the place. Lambda is a cat café — you show up, cat appears, you leave, you pay per cuddle. None is "best": the adopter, the cattery client, and the café visitor are all correct for their life circumstances. Say that sentence in an interview and the EC2-vs-Lambda question is over.

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

A teammate says "we should move to Lambda because it's more modern than ECS." What's the senior reframe?

Why: the axes that actually decide it: traffic shape (spiky → Lambda's scale-to-zero shines; steady → you're renting per-invocation markup for nothing), execution model (15-min limit, cold starts vs long-lived connections/pools), and team ops appetite. "Modern" isn't an axis. Bonus nuance: Lambda + RDS needs RDS Proxy because per-invocation connections savage Postgres — the pooling lesson from the postgres module, wearing cloud clothes.

LEVEL UP — what interviewers listen for
  • Regions (isolated geographies) → Availability Zones (independent data centers within one) — "multi-AZ = survives a building; multi-region = survives a city, at 10× the complexity". Most products need the first, not the second.
  • VPC in one sentence: your private network — public subnets for things the internet reaches (ALB), private subnets for everything else (app, DB). That's 90% of network questions.
  • "Undifferentiated heavy lifting" is the phrase that explains WHY managed services exist — you sell cat photos, not database backups.

02

The twelve services — each in one honest paragraph

the working vocabulary, aisle by aisle

Compute. EC2: virtual machines — full control, steady workloads, you patch. ECS on Fargate: hand over a container image + CPU/RAM spec; AWS runs and scales it — the default home for a Node API in 2026. Lambda: functions triggered by events (HTTP, queue messages, S3 uploads, cron) — spiky work, glue, and zero-baseline economics.

Storage & data. S3: infinitely-scaling object storage for files — uploads, builds, backups, static sites; you'll use it weekly forever. RDS (Postgres): your database with backups, patching, Multi-AZ failover, and read replicas managed — everything from the postgres module, operated for you. ElastiCache (Redis): managed cache/session store/rate-limit counters. DynamoDB: AWS's own key-value store — single-digit-ms at any scale, in exchange for modeling around access patterns; the "when not Postgres" answer.

Traffic & edge. Route 53: DNS. ALB: the load balancer — TLS termination, health checks, spreading traffic across app copies. CloudFront: the CDN — your static assets cached in 400 cities, plus TLS at the edge.

Messaging & the layers. SQS: the queue — decouple slow work from request paths (the email-sending, PDF-rendering answer — and the fix for node-backend's noon mystery). SNS/EventBridge: fan-out and event routing. Over everything: IAM (§04) and CloudWatch (§05).

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

Users upload 200MB videos through your Node API to S3. The right architecture?

Why: proxying 200MB through your API burns its bandwidth, memory, and ALB timeouts for zero added value — the presigned URL (a time-limited, permission-scoped upload pass) lets S3 do what it's for, and the S3-event → Lambda/SQS chain handles thumbnails/scanning asynchronously. This pattern — "hand the client a scoped pass, let the managed service do the heavy lifting, react to its events" — is the single most reusable AWS design move, and this exact question is asked constantly.

LEVEL UP — what interviewers listen for
  • S3 nuances worth one line each: versioning (undelete), lifecycle rules (auto-archive to Glacier), and "S3 is not a filesystem — it's a key-value store for blobs".
  • SQS semantics: at-least-once delivery + visibility timeout → consumers must be idempotent (the frontend course's retry lesson, server-side) — and a dead-letter queue catches poison messages.
  • DynamoDB vs RDS in one breath: "known access patterns at massive scale vs flexible queries with relations — most products are the second until proven otherwise."

03

Three architectures — the same app at three life stages

intermediate → assembling the twelve into real shapes

The cat café app (React front, Node API, Postgres) at three honest life stages. Stage 1 — the scrappy start (≈$30–60/mo): one EC2 instance running the API (PM2), RDS single-AZ, S3 + CloudFront for the frontend build. It's "a VPS with better neighbors" — and it's genuinely correct for a new product. Its known sins: deploys drop requests, one AZ, one instance = one 3am pager.

Stage 2 — the grown-up default (≈$200–500/mo): the frontend unchanged (S3+CloudFront scale infinitely for pennies); the API as containers on ECS Fargate (2+ tasks across AZs) behind an ALB with health checks; RDS Multi-AZ + a read replica; Redis for sessions/cache (statelessness unlocked horizontal scaling — the node module's auth design pays off here); SQS + a worker service for slow jobs. This is the architecture that carries most products from launch to acquisition.

Stage 3 — event-driven growth: same skeleton, plus: auto-scaling policies on ECS (scale on CPU/queue depth), CloudFront in front of the API too (cached GETs at the edge), EventBridge routing domain events (adoption.created → notify, analytics, email fan-out), and aggressive Lambda adoption for the spiky edges. The skeleton doesn't get replaced — it gets relieved: each piece that hurts gets a managed pressure valve.

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

In stage 2, sessions moved from the API's memory to Redis. What breaks if you skip that — and what AWS feature papers over it badly?

Why: in-memory state is the thing that forbids horizontal scaling — the second instance is a different universe. Sticky sessions pin users to tasks (uneven load, sessions die with the task, deploys log users out) — name it as the tempting anti-pattern. JWTs partially dodge it (stateless auth) but the auth module's revocation trade still applies, and non-auth state (carts, rate limits) needs a home anyway. "Stateless app tier, state in Redis/RDS" is the sentence the whole stage-2 diagram hangs from.

LEVEL UP — what interviewers listen for
  • Deploy story for stage 2, in a breath: build once in CI → push to ECR → ECS rolling update behind the ALB's health checks → SIGTERM-graceful tasks drain — zero-downtime with all the pieces you already know.
  • Where Terraform/CDK enters: the whole diagram as reviewable code — "the environment is a pull request". One sentence; deep IaC is its own interview.
  • The honest stage-1 defense: "I'd start on stage 1 and I can name exactly which pain triggers each stage-2 upgrade" reads as far more senior than defaulting to microservices on day one.

04

IAM without tears — roles, not keys

the section that prevents your worst production day

IAM's model in four words: who · can-do · what · on-which. Policies are JSON statements (Effect/Action/Resource) attached to identities. The two identities that matter: users (humans — should be rare, MFA'd, and mostly replaced by SSO) and roles — identities that services assume temporarily. The golden rule that prevents the classic disaster: code never holds long-lived keys. Your ECS task gets a role; AWS injects short-lived, auto-rotated credentials; the SDK just works. A leaked .env with AKIA-keys is how startups end up mining bitcoin for strangers — a role can't leak that way.

Least privilege is the design discipline: the API's role can read/write its S3 bucket and its queue — not s3:* on *. When something's denied, you widen deliberately, one action at a time. Tedious exactly once, priceless the day any component is compromised: the blast radius is the role's permissions, not your account.

api-task-role.json — least privilege, annotated
{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    {
      "Sid": "UploadsBucketOnly",
      "Effect": "Allow",
      "Action": ["s3:GetObject", "s3:PutObject"],
      "Resource": "arn:aws:s3:::cafe-uploads/*"
    },
    {
      "Sid": "OwnQueueOnly",
      "Effect": "Allow",
      "Action": ["sqs:SendMessage"],
      "Resource": "arn:aws:sqs:ap-south-1:123456789012:cafe-jobs"
    }
  ]
}

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

A dependency you ship gets compromised and runs attacker code inside your API container. Which prior decision most limits the damage?

Why: the attacker inherits exactly what the compromised process could do — that's why the role's scope IS the blast radius. TLS protects transit (irrelevant here — they're inside), WAF filters inbound (they're already in), replication protects against hardware (not attackers with credentials). This supply-chain scenario appears in the fullstack course's security bank too — the answer is the same from both directions: least privilege turned a catastrophe into an incident.

LEVEL UP — what interviewers listen for
  • Secrets that aren't IAM (DB passwords, API keys): Secrets Manager/Parameter Store, fetched at boot by the role — the .env file's grown-up replacement, closing the node module's config story.
  • Presigned URLs (§02) are IAM in miniature: a time-boxed capability minted from your role's permissions — one concept, two sections.
  • "IAM is deny-by-default; explicit Deny beats any Allow" — the two evaluation rules that answer most policy-puzzle questions.

05

Running it — knowing before the users tweet

intermediate → logs, metrics, alarms, and the deploy loop

CloudWatch is three tools sharing a name. Logs: every container's stdout (the node module's structured JSON) lands in log groups — searchable with Logs Insights; request ids make one user's journey traceable. Metrics: numbers over time — the free ones (CPU, ALB 5xx rate, target response time, queue depth, DB connections) plus your custom RED metrics per route. Alarms: thresholds on metrics that page you — and the discipline is alarming on symptoms users feel (5xx rate, p99 latency, queue age) rather than causes (CPU%), because causes without symptoms are just weather.

The loop that makes it real: deploy (rolling, health-checked) → watch the release-annotated dashboards → alarm fires → logs filtered by request id → fix or roll back (the previous task definition is one click). Health checks split exactly as in the node module: liveness ("restart me") vs readiness ("don't route to me") — ALBs act on the second; conflating them causes the restart-storm-during-DB-blip incident everyone has once.

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

Which alarm set is right for the stage-2 café app — pick the SYMPTOM-first set?

Why: a CPU spike serving fast responses needs no human; a 5xx spike at 2% CPU absolutely does. Symptom alarms have near-zero false positives (users ARE hurting) and cause metrics stay on dashboards for diagnosis, not paging. "Queue oldest-message age" over "queue length" is the connoisseur pick — length can be high and healthy; age growing means consumers are losing. Alert fatigue from cause-alarms is why on-call rotations burn out — saying that shows you've lived it.

LEVEL UP — what interviewers listen for
  • Auto-scaling policies read like alarms too: target-tracking on CPU or ALB requests-per-target for the API; queue depth for workers — "scale on the same signals you'd page on, one notch earlier".
  • Cold rollback beats hot debugging at 2am: previous image + previous task definition, then diagnose in daylight — the frontend course's "mitigate before diagnose", infra edition.
  • X-Ray/tracing in one line: request ids across service hops, automated — name it, don't tour it.

The bill — cost as an architecture property

advanced → the four line items and the classic surprises

AWS bills for four things, roughly in this order for a typical app: compute time (EC2/Fargate hours, Lambda GB-seconds), database (instance hours + storage + Multi-AZ doubling), data transfer OUT (the sneaky one — into AWS is free, out to the internet costs, cross-AZ costs both ways), and storage (cheap until you never delete anything). The classic surprises are all transfer: NAT gateway hourly + per-GB fees for private-subnet internet access, cross-AZ chatter between services, and un-cached images served from S3 instead of CloudFront.

Cost discipline that fits in one paragraph: tag everything (know which feature spends), budgets + billing alarms from day one (the free insurance nobody buys), right-size after measuring (the t3.large at 4% CPU), Savings Plans once steady-state is known (~30–40% off compute you'll run anyway), lifecycle-expire old S3 data and logs, and put CloudFront in front of anything users download twice. And the architecture-level truth: the cheapest request is the one a cache absorbed — CloudFront and Redis are cost tools as much as latency tools.

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

The bill doubled this month. Traffic didn't. The three most likely suspects, in order?

Why: method first — Cost Explorer's group-by-service + the tag report turns a mystery into a diff; the trio above covers most real doublings. Compromise (option 3) is real but announces itself as GPU instances in regions you've never used — worth one glance, not panic. Multi-AZ doubling is visible the month you enable it, not randomly. "Cost is observable like latency — measure, don't speculate" is the transferable sentence.

LEVEL UP — what interviewers listen for
  • Lambda's economics in one line: free at zero, brilliant at spiky, and at sustained high throughput a steady Fargate service is often cheaper — do the arithmetic, not the ideology.
  • "We put a billing alarm at 2× expected on day one" is the cheapest sentence that sounds like scar tissue — because it always is.
  • The interview close: cost, latency, and resilience are the same three-way trade — every cache, replica, and AZ is a position in it. Frame answers that way and infra questions become design questions.

// wrap up · where this gets asked

In the interview

AWS questions test judgment about managed services, not console trivia.

  • "How would you deploy this app?" — draw stage 2 from memory, then narrate stage 1 as where you'd actually start and what pain triggers each upgrade. The staging story IS the senior answer.
  • "EC2 vs ECS vs Lambda?" — the slider + workload shape, with the cattery analogy if the room is friendly. Rapid-fire pairs: Full-Stack in 48 · AWS map and · scaling & the bill.
  • "How do you secure service-to-service access?" — roles-not-keys, least privilege, secrets manager, presigned URLs. Four phrases, full marks.
  • Next module: system-design-backend — where these boxes become the vocabulary for designs at whiteboard scale.

Draw stage 2 until your hand knows it. The map is small; the confidence of holding it is enormous.
