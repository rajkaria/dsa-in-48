// module 10 two hundred services, twelve that matter

AWS as a map, not a catalog.

AWS feels unlearnable because people study it as an alphabet of product names. It isn't — it's five questions every deployment answers (where does code run, where do files live, where does data live, how does traffic arrive, how do parts talk), each with a managed answer. Learn the questions, and two hundred services collapse into a map with twelve landmarks.

This module builds the map, tours the twelve, assembles them into three honest reference architectures for a MERN app, then covers the two things everyone learns by incident instead: IAM and the bill. The cats are deploying their café app. Against all advice. 🐈

01

The mental map — five questions, five aisles

beginner → the frame that makes everything else attachable

Every app you've ever deployed answers the same five questions. Compute: where does my code run? Object storage: where do files/uploads/builds live? Data: where does structured state live? Networking/edge: how does traffic find me, get balanced, get cached? Messaging: how do parts talk without holding hands? AWS is those five aisles, plus two cross-cutting layers over everything: IAM (who may do what) and observability (what's happening).

The second half of the frame is the managed-ness slider: for each aisle you choose how much you operate yourself. Compute runs from EC2 (a VM you patch) → ECS/Fargate (containers, no hosts) → Lambda (functions, no anything). More managed = less ops and less control, usually more per-unit cost and less to go wrong. Nearly every AWS decision is this slider wearing a product name.

PROFESSOR WHISKERS EXPLAINSFeeding a cat, at every point on the managed-ness slider: EC2 is adopting the cat — total control, you handle vet visits at 3am. ECS/Fargate is a cattery — you specify the diet, they run the place. Lambda is a cat café — you show up, cat appears, you leave, you pay per cuddle. None is "best": the adopter, the cattery client, and the café visitor are all correct for their life circumstances. Say that sentence in an interview and the EC2-vs-Lambda question is over.

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

A teammate says "we should move to Lambda because it's more modern than ECS." What's the senior reframe?

Why: the axes that actually decide it: traffic shape (spiky → Lambda's scale-to-zero shines; steady → you're renting per-invocation markup for nothing), execution model (15-min limit, cold starts vs long-lived connections/pools), and team ops appetite. "Modern" isn't an axis. Bonus nuance: Lambda + RDS needs RDS Proxy because per-invocation connections savage Postgres — the pooling lesson from the postgres module, wearing cloud clothes.

LEVEL UP — what interviewers listen for
  • Regions (isolated geographies) → Availability Zones (independent data centers within one) — "multi-AZ = survives a building; multi-region = survives a city, at 10× the complexity". Most products need the first, not the second.
  • VPC in one sentence: your private network — public subnets for things the internet reaches (ALB), private subnets for everything else (app, DB). That's 90% of network questions.
  • "Undifferentiated heavy lifting" is the phrase that explains WHY managed services exist — you sell cat photos, not database backups.

02

The twelve services — each in one honest paragraph

the working vocabulary, aisle by aisle

Compute. EC2: virtual machines — full control, steady workloads, you patch. ECS on Fargate: hand over a container image + CPU/RAM spec; AWS runs and scales it — the default home for a Node API in 2026. Lambda: functions triggered by events (HTTP, queue messages, S3 uploads, cron) — spiky work, glue, and zero-baseline economics.

Storage & data. S3: infinitely-scaling object storage for files — uploads, builds, backups, static sites; you'll use it weekly forever. RDS (Postgres): your database with backups, patching, Multi-AZ failover, and read replicas managed — everything from the postgres module, operated for you. ElastiCache (Redis): managed cache/session store/rate-limit counters. DynamoDB: AWS's own key-value store — single-digit-ms at any scale, in exchange for modeling around access patterns; the "when not Postgres" answer.

Traffic & edge. Route 53: DNS. ALB: the load balancer — TLS termination, health checks, spreading traffic across app copies. CloudFront: the CDN — your static assets cached in 400 cities, plus TLS at the edge.

Messaging & the layers. SQS: the queue — decouple slow work from request paths (the email-sending, PDF-rendering answer — and the fix for node-backend's noon mystery). SNS/EventBridge: fan-out and event routing. Over everything: IAM (§04) and CloudWatch (§05).

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

Users upload 200MB videos through your Node API to S3. The right architecture?

Why: proxying 200MB through your API burns its bandwidth, memory, and ALB timeouts for zero added value — the presigned URL (a time-limited, permission-scoped upload pass) lets S3 do what it's for, and the S3-event → Lambda/SQS chain handles thumbnails/scanning asynchronously. This pattern — "hand the client a scoped pass, let the managed service do the heavy lifting, react to its events" — is the single most reusable AWS design move, and this exact question is asked constantly.

LEVEL UP — what interviewers listen for
  • S3 nuances worth one line each: versioning (undelete), lifecycle rules (auto-archive to Glacier), and "S3 is not a filesystem — it's a key-value store for blobs".
  • SQS semantics: at-least-once delivery + visibility timeout → consumers must be idempotent (the frontend course's retry lesson, server-side) — and a dead-letter queue catches poison messages.
  • DynamoDB vs RDS in one breath: "known access patterns at massive scale vs flexible queries with relations — most products are the second until proven otherwise."

03

Three architectures — the same app at three life stages

intermediate → assembling the twelve into real shapes

The cat café app (React front, Node API, Postgres) at three honest life stages. Stage 1 — the scrappy start (≈$30–60/mo): one EC2 instance running the API (PM2), RDS single-AZ, S3 + CloudFront for the frontend build. It's "a VPS with better neighbors" — and it's genuinely correct for a new product. Its known sins: deploys drop requests, one AZ, one instance = one 3am pager.

Stage 2 — the grown-up default (≈$200–500/mo): the frontend unchanged (S3+CloudFront scale infinitely for pennies); the API as containers on ECS Fargate (2+ tasks across AZs) behind an ALB with health checks; RDS Multi-AZ + a read replica; Redis for sessions/cache (statelessness unlocked horizontal scaling — the node module's auth design pays off here); SQS + a worker service for slow jobs. This is the architecture that carries most products from launch to acquisition.

Stage 3 — event-driven growth: same skeleton, plus: auto-scaling policies on ECS (scale on CPU/queue depth), CloudFront in front of the API too (cached GETs at the edge), EventBridge routing domain events (adoption.created → notify, analytics, email fan-out), and aggressive Lambda adoption for the spiky edges. The skeleton doesn't get replaced — it gets relieved: each piece that hurts gets a managed pressure valve.

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

In stage 2, sessions moved from the API's memory to Redis. What breaks if you skip that — and what AWS feature papers over it badly?

Why: in-memory state is the thing that forbids horizontal scaling — the second instance is a different universe. Sticky sessions pin users to tasks (uneven load, sessions die with the task, deploys log users out) — name it as the tempting anti-pattern. JWTs partially dodge it (stateless auth) but the auth module's revocation trade still applies, and non-auth state (carts, rate limits) needs a home anyway. "Stateless app tier, state in Redis/RDS" is the sentence the whole stage-2 diagram hangs from.

LEVEL UP — what interviewers listen for
  • Deploy story for stage 2, in a breath: build once in CI → push to ECR → ECS rolling update behind the ALB's health checks → SIGTERM-graceful tasks drain — zero-downtime with all the pieces you already know.
  • Where Terraform/CDK enters: the whole diagram as reviewable code — "the environment is a pull request". One sentence; deep IaC is its own interview.
  • The honest stage-1 defense: "I'd start on stage 1 and I can name exactly which pain triggers each stage-2 upgrade" reads as far more senior than defaulting to microservices on day one.

04

IAM without tears — roles, not keys

the section that prevents your worst production day

IAM's model in four words: who · can-do · what · on-which. Policies are JSON statements (Effect/Action/Resource) attached to identities. The two identities that matter: users (humans — should be rare, MFA'd, and mostly replaced by SSO) and roles — identities that services assume temporarily. The golden rule that prevents the classic disaster: code never holds long-lived keys. Your ECS task gets a role; AWS injects short-lived, auto-rotated credentials; the SDK just works. A leaked .env with AKIA-keys is how startups end up mining bitcoin for strangers — a role can't leak that way.

Least privilege is the design discipline: the API's role can read/write its S3 bucket and its queue — not s3:* on *. When something's denied, you widen deliberately, one action at a time. Tedious exactly once, priceless the day any component is compromised: the blast radius is the role's permissions, not your account.

api-task-role.json — least privilege, annotated
{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    {
      "Sid": "UploadsBucketOnly",
      "Effect": "Allow",
      "Action": ["s3:GetObject", "s3:PutObject"],
      "Resource": "arn:aws:s3:::cafe-uploads/*"
    },
    {
      "Sid": "OwnQueueOnly",
      "Effect": "Allow",
      "Action": ["sqs:SendMessage"],
      "Resource": "arn:aws:sqs:ap-south-1:123456789012:cafe-jobs"
    }
  ]
}

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

A dependency you ship gets compromised and runs attacker code inside your API container. Which prior decision most limits the damage?

Why: the attacker inherits exactly what the compromised process could do — that's why the role's scope IS the blast radius. TLS protects transit (irrelevant here — they're inside), WAF filters inbound (they're already in), replication protects against hardware (not attackers with credentials). This supply-chain scenario appears in the fullstack course's security bank too — the answer is the same from both directions: least privilege turned a catastrophe into an incident.

LEVEL UP — what interviewers listen for
  • Secrets that aren't IAM (DB passwords, API keys): Secrets Manager/Parameter Store, fetched at boot by the role — the .env file's grown-up replacement, closing the node module's config story.
  • Presigned URLs (§02) are IAM in miniature: a time-boxed capability minted from your role's permissions — one concept, two sections.
  • "IAM is deny-by-default; explicit Deny beats any Allow" — the two evaluation rules that answer most policy-puzzle questions.

05

IAM, mechanically — policy evaluation & assumed roles

intermediate → how a request is actually judged

Section 04 gave the philosophy; here's the machine. Every API call is judged against all applicable policies by three rules in strict order: (1) explicit Deny anywhere → denied, final — no Allow outranks it; (2) otherwise, any matching Allow → allowed; (3) otherwise → implicit deny (the default state of everything). Policies are JSON statements — Effect, Action (s3:GetObject), Resource (an ARN, wildcardable), optional Condition (IP, tags, encryption state). That's the whole calculus; everything else is which policies apply (identity-attached, resource-attached like bucket policies, permission boundaries, org SCPs — all funnel into the same three rules).

AssumeRole is the verb behind "roles, never keys": STS issues temporary credentials (minutes-hours) for a role, given the role's trust policy agrees you may wear it. EC2 instance profiles, Lambda execution roles, cross-account access, and CI deployments via OIDC federation — all the same handshake: prove who you are, borrow a costume with its own permissions, let it expire.

policy-judge.js — run it (the evaluator, complete)
// the three-rule judgment, implemented
const match = (pattern, value) =>
  new RegExp('^' + pattern.split('*').map(s => s.replace(/[.+?^${}()|[\]\\]/g, '\\$&')).join('.*') + '$').test(value);

function judge(policies, action, resource) {
  const applicable = policies.flatMap(p => p.statements)
    .filter(s => match(s.action, action) && match(s.resource, resource));
  if (applicable.some(s => s.effect === 'Deny'))  return 'DENY  (explicit — nothing overrides it)';
  if (applicable.some(s => s.effect === 'Allow')) return 'ALLOW';
  return 'DENY  (implicit — nobody said yes)';
}

const policies = [
  { name: 'app-role', statements: [
    { effect: 'Allow', action: 's3:GetObject', resource: 'arn:aws:s3:::cat-photos/*' },
    { effect: 'Allow', action: 's3:PutObject', resource: 'arn:aws:s3:::cat-photos/uploads/*' },
  ]},
  { name: 'org-guardrail', statements: [
    { effect: 'Deny', action: 's3:*', resource: 'arn:aws:s3:::cat-photos/vet-records/*' },
  ]},
];

const cases = [
  ['s3:GetObject', 'arn:aws:s3:::cat-photos/mochi.jpg'],
  ['s3:PutObject', 'arn:aws:s3:::cat-photos/uploads/new.jpg'],
  ['s3:PutObject', 'arn:aws:s3:::cat-photos/mochi.jpg'],          // outside uploads/
  ['s3:GetObject', 'arn:aws:s3:::cat-photos/vet-records/x.pdf'],  // guardrail!
  ['s3:DeleteObject', 'arn:aws:s3:::cat-photos/mochi.jpg'],       // nobody allowed it
];
for (const [action, res] of cases)
  console.log(`${action.padEnd(16)} ${res.split(':::')[1].padEnd(28)} → ${judge(policies, action, res)}`);
console.log('\nnote the vet-records GET: an Allow matched AND a Deny matched — Deny wins.');
console.log('that is why org guardrails are Denies: they cannot be accidentally out-allowed.');
five judgments, three rules — the vet-records case is the whole exam

    06
    

      
VPC networking — subnets, routes & the two firewalls

      
intermediate → where packets may go, decided by four tables

    

  

  

    
A VPC is your private network: a CIDR block (say 10.0.0.0/16) carved into subnets per availability zone. A subnet is public or private by exactly one distinction: does its route table send 0.0.0.0/0 to an Internet Gateway (two-way internet) or to a NAT Gateway (outbound-only — the pattern for app servers that call APIs but must not be reachable)? The standard stage-2 topology: ALB in public subnets, app + database in private ones, NAT for the app's outbound. Databases get no internet route at all.

    
Two firewalls filter what routes allow: Security Groups — attached to resources, stateful (a reply to an allowed request is automatically allowed), allow-rules only, and they can reference each other ("db-sg allows 5432 from app-sg" — identity-based, survives IP churn); NACLs — subnet-level, stateless, with deny rules — the coarse outer fence you mostly leave alone.

    

      

        
packet-walk.js — run it (route table + SG evaluation)

        
const subnets = {
  'public-a':  { cidr: '10.0.1.0/24', route: { '0.0.0.0/0': 'igw' } },
  'private-a': { cidr: '10.0.10.0/24', route: { '0.0.0.0/0': 'nat' } },
  'db-a':      { cidr: '10.0.20.0/24', route: {} },                    // no internet route
};
const sgs = {
  'alb-sg': [{ port: 443, from: '0.0.0.0/0' }],
  'app-sg': [{ port: 3000, from: 'alb-sg' }],          // SG references SG!
  'db-sg':  [{ port: 5432, from: 'app-sg' }],
};

function inbound(target, port, source) {
  const ok = sgs[target].some(r => r.port === port && (r.from === source || r.from === '0.0.0.0/0'));
  return `${source} → ${target}:${port}  ${ok ? '✓ allowed' : '✗ no rule → dropped'}`;
}
console.log('— inbound, judged by security groups —');
console.log(inbound('alb-sg', 443, '0.0.0.0/0'));
console.log(inbound('app-sg', 3000, 'alb-sg'));
console.log(inbound('app-sg', 3000, '0.0.0.0/0'));     // internet straight to app?
console.log(inbound('db-sg', 5432, 'app-sg'));
console.log(inbound('db-sg', 5432, 'alb-sg'));          // ALB to DB?

console.log('\n— outbound, judged by route tables —');
for (const [name, s] of Object.entries(subnets)) {
  const via = s.route['0.0.0.0/0'];
  console.log(`${name.padEnd(10)} to api.stripe.com → ` + (
    via === 'igw' ? 'internet gateway (and the internet can route BACK in)' :
    via === 'nat' ? 'NAT gateway (outbound only — no unsolicited inbound)' :
    'NO ROUTE — the database cannot phone anyone. Correct.'));
}

    07
    

      
The compute spectrum — EC2 to Lambda, priced honestly

      
intermediate → workload shape picks the runtime, not fashion

    

  

  

    
Section 02 introduced the slider; this section gives it numbers and edges. EC2: a rented computer — max control, you patch it, you pay while it idles. ECS on Fargate: containers without servers — the steady-state default; you say CPU/memory/count, AWS finds the metal. Lambda: functions per-request — zero idle cost, automatic scale, but cold starts (a paused runtime spinning up: ~100ms–1s+, worse with VPC attachment and heavy runtimes), 15-minute max, and per-invocation pricing that crosses over: at sustained load, always-on containers get cheaper than a million-invocations bill. App Runner / Elastic Beanstalk: PaaS wrappers — fine starts, fewer knobs.

    
The decision inputs that matter: traffic shape (spiky/rare → Lambda; steady → containers), duration (long jobs can't Lambda), latency floor (cold starts on the checkout path?), state (websockets §nb-13 want long-lived processes), and ops appetite. Say those five and the "which compute?" question answers itself in front of the interviewer.

    

      

        
crossover-math.js — run it (Lambda vs Fargate, priced)

        
// rough public-ish prices (illustrative, the SHAPE is the lesson)
const lambda = (reqPerMo, msPerReq, gb) => {
  const gbSeconds = reqPerMo * (msPerReq / 1000) * gb;
  return reqPerMo * 0.0000002 + gbSeconds * 0.0000167;
};
const fargate = (vcpu, gb) => (vcpu * 0.04048 + gb * 0.004445) * 730;  // always-on, hourly

console.log('API: 200ms/req at 1GB — Lambda vs one 0.5vCPU/1GB Fargate task:\n');
console.log('  req/month        Lambda          Fargate');
for (const req of [1e5, 1e6, 5e6, 2e7, 1e8]) {
  const l = lambda(req, 200, 1), f = fargate(0.5, 1);
  console.log(`  ${req.toExponential(0).padEnd(8)}   $${l.toFixed(2).padStart(9)}   $${f.toFixed(2).padStart(9)}   ${l < f ? '← Lambda wins' : '← container wins'}`);
}
console.log('\nthe crossover is real and it is BORING traffic that finds it:');
console.log('spiky/rare → pay per use. steady hum → pay for the box.');

    08
    

      
S3, deeply — classes, lifecycle & the URL-shaped passes

      
intermediate → the object store as a system component

    

  

  

    
S3 is a key→object store (no real folders — the slashes are cosplay) with eleven-nines durability and, since 2020, strong read-after-write consistency (the eventual-consistency folklore is dated — knowing that dates you). The system-design power comes from three features. Storage classes + lifecycle rules: Standard → Infrequent Access → Glacier tiers, with rules that demote objects on a schedule — logs that are hot for a week and archival forever shouldn't pay hot prices for year two. Presigned URLs (§02's scoped passes): uploads and downloads go client↔S3 directly, so 100MB videos never transit your Node server (backpressure problem deleted, not solved). Event notifications: an upload can trigger a Lambda/queue — "thumbnail on upload" without polling.

    

      

        
lifecycle-bill.js — run it (classes + lifecycle, priced)

        
// 10TB of logs/month, hot for 30 days, kept 1 year for compliance
const TB = 1024;             // GB
const prices = { standard: 0.023, ia: 0.0125, glacierIR: 0.004, deep: 0.00099 }; // $/GB-mo

const strategies = {
  'all Standard forever': (mo) => {
    let cost = 0;                                           // data ACCUMULATES —
    for (let m = 1; m <= mo; m++) cost += 10 * TB * m * prices.standard;
    return cost;                                            // month 12 stores 120TB
  },
  'lifecycle: 30d Std → IA → 90d Glacier-IR': (mo) => {
    let cost = 0;
    for (let m = 1; m <= mo; m++) {
      cost += 10 * TB * prices.standard;                    // this month's fresh logs
      if (m > 1) cost += 10 * TB * Math.min(m - 1, 2) * prices.ia;   // months 2-3
      if (m > 3) cost += 10 * TB * (m - 3) * prices.glacierIR;       // 4+
    }
    return cost;
  },
};
for (const [name, fn] of Object.entries(strategies))
  console.log(`${name.padEnd(42)} 12-month total: $${Math.round(fn(12)).toLocaleString()}`);

console.log('\nsame bytes, same durability — the lifecycle RULE is the savings.');
console.log('the catch to name: retrieval fees + minimum storage durations on cold tiers;');
console.log('Glacier is for data you expect NOT to read. Reading it all back monthly un-saves everything.');

      

      
one lifecycle rule ≈ halving the bill — and the retrieval-fee caveat is the senior half

RDS, Aurora & DynamoDB — the managed-data decision

      
intermediate → relational vs single-table, without the tribalism

    

  

  

    
RDS is managed Postgres/MySQL — everything the postgres module taught, with AWS running backups, patches, Multi-AZ failover, and read replicas. Aurora reimplements the storage layer (six-way replicated, storage auto-grows, replicas share it) — better failover and scale headroom at a premium; Aurora Serverless adds per-second elasticity. DynamoDB is a different animal: a managed key-value/wide-column store where you get O(1)-ish reads/writes at any scale — if you know your access patterns up front. Single-table design inverts everything §pg-02 taught: instead of normalized entities joined at query time, you pre-join by storing items so each access pattern is one partition-key lookup. No ad-hoc queries, no joins — that's the price of horizontal infinity.

    
The decision heuristic that survives contact: relational until access patterns are few, known, and scale-critical. Dynamo shines for carts, sessions, feature flags, IoT firehoses; it punishes evolving products that suddenly need "show me all orders over $50 last month" (that's a scan, or an export to analytics).

    

      

        
single-table.js — run it (the same data, both shapes)

        
// access pattern: "load a cat AND its recent visits" — one page, hot path
// RELATIONAL: two tables, a join (or two queries) at read time
const cats = [{ id: 'c1', name: 'Mochi' }];
const visits = [
  { catId: 'c1', date: '2026-08-01', note: 'checkup' },
  { catId: 'c1', date: '2026-08-05', note: 'shots' },
];
console.log('relational read: SELECT … JOIN visits — flexible, any future question answerable\n');

// DYNAMO single-table: pre-joined by KEY DESIGN — entity type lives in the sort key
const table = [
  { PK: 'CAT#c1', SK: 'PROFILE',            name: 'Mochi' },
  { PK: 'CAT#c1', SK: 'VISIT#2026-08-01',   note: 'checkup' },
  { PK: 'CAT#c1', SK: 'VISIT#2026-08-05',   note: 'shots' },
];
// Query(PK = CAT#c1) → profile + visits, ONE request, already sorted by SK
const page = table.filter(i => i.PK === 'CAT#c1');
console.log('dynamo read: Query(PK="CAT#c1") returns', page.length, 'items in one round trip:');
page.forEach(i => console.log(`  ${i.SK.padEnd(18)} ${i.name ?? i.note}`));

console.log('\nnow the question the design DIDN\'T plan for: "all visits in August, any cat"');
console.log('relational: WHERE date BETWEEN … (an index away).');
console.log('dynamo: full table Scan (reads EVERY item) — or a new GSI shaped for it.');
console.log('→ Dynamo trades query freedom for guaranteed-flat latency. Know which you\'re buying.');

      

      
one Query vs one Scan — the whole single-table bargain in two lines

      

    

    

      
// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

      
A startup's product is evolving weekly; queries change constantly; scale is ~thousands of users. A consultant proposes DynamoDB single-table "for scale". Assessment?

      

        
        
        
        
      

      
Why: Dynamo's constraint (patterns-first) is exactly what an exploring product can't provide — you'd be buying web-scale latency guarantees you don't need with flexibility you desperately do. The reverse mistake is real too (Postgres melting under a true single-pattern firehose) — the skill being tested is matching the store to the certainty of the access patterns, not to ambition. "Postgres until the patterns crystallize" is the calibrated answer.

    • 
        
  • Dynamo vocabulary that signals depth: partition key design against hot partitions, GSIs as "alternate pre-joins", on-demand vs provisioned capacity, DAX for read-through caching.
    • 
        
  • ElastiCache (Redis) completes the data tier: cache-aside for hot reads, plus its side jobs — rate limits (§nb-11), pub/sub (§nb-13), sessions (§nb-12). One box, four cameos.
    • 
        
  • "Big analytical questions" belong to neither: export to the analytics stack (Athena/Redshift) — OLTP stores answering OLAP questions is its own outage genre.

SQS, SNS & EventBridge — decoupling, with delivery honesty

      
intermediate → queues vs fan-out vs event routing, and the retry rules

    

  

  

    
Three services, three shapes. SQS — a queue: one producer side, one consumer pool; work sits until someone takes it; scale consumers to drain faster. SNS — pub/sub fan-out: one event, N subscribers each get a copy (email service AND analytics AND audit — none knowing about the others). EventBridge — an event bus with content-based routing rules ("orders over $500 → fraud check"), schemas, and third-party sources; SNS with a brain, for event-driven architectures. The standard combo: SNS/EventBridge fans out into SQS queues per consumer, so each consumer gets buffering + retries independently.

    
The honesty part every interview probes: SQS standard is at-least-once — a consumer that takes a message but crashes before deleting it will see the message redelivered after the visibility timeout. That's not a bug; it's the contract. Consequences: handlers must be idempotent (dedupe by message/business id — nb's dedupe map, now mandatory), poison messages need a DLQ (dead-letter queue after N failed receives — quarantine, alert, inspect), and ordering isn't promised (FIFO queues exist, at a throughput price, for when it truly is).

    

      

        
at-least-once.js — run it (crash, redelivery, idempotent survival)

        
// an SQS-ish queue: visibility timeout + receive counts + DLQ after 3
const queue = [{ id: 'm1', body: 'charge order#42 — $19', receives: 0, visibleAt: 0 }];
const dlq = [];
const processed = new Set();                       // idempotency ledger (by business key)

function receiveAndHandle(t, { crash = false } = {}) {
  const msg = queue.find(m => m.visibleAt <= t);
  if (!msg) return console.log(`t=${t}s: queue quiet`);
  msg.receives++;
  msg.visibleAt = t + 30;                          // invisible while being worked
  if (msg.receives > 3) {
    queue.splice(queue.indexOf(msg), 1); dlq.push(msg);
    return console.log(`t=${t}s: receive #${msg.receives} → over limit → DLQ + alert 🚨`);
  }
  console.log(`t=${t}s: receive #${msg.receives} of "${msg.body}"`);
  if (crash) {
    processed.add('order#42');
    console.log('        charged $19, ledger updated');
    return console.log('        💥 consumer crashed BEFORE deleting the message');
  }
  if (processed.has('order#42')) {
    console.log('        already-processed ledger hit → skip charge, just delete ✓ (idempotent!)');
  } else {
    processed.add('order#42');
    console.log('        charged $19, ledger updated');
  }
  queue.splice(queue.indexOf(msg), 1);             // the DELETE — the real ack
  console.log('        message deleted — done');
}

receiveAndHandle(0, { crash: true });              // charge happens, delete doesn't!
receiveAndHandle(10);                              // still invisible…
receiveAndHandle(31);                              // visibility expired → REDELIVERED
console.log('\nwithout the ledger, order#42 is charged twice. At-least-once means');
console.log('"design for the duplicate" — the queue kept its promise; naive code broke its own.');

      

      
the crash-then-redelivery is the whole contract — the ledger is the fix

      

    

    

      
// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

      
Order events must reach: email service, warehouse, analytics. New consumers get added quarterly. The wiring?

      

        
        
        
        
      

      
Why: option B is the famous trap — a queue delivers each message to ONE consumer in the pool; the three services would steal each other's events. Fan-out-then-buffer gives broadcast semantics AND per-consumer queue benefits. Option A couples the API to every consumer's uptime (checkout fails because analytics is down — the exact coupling messaging exists to delete). The "add a consumer without touching producers" clause is what makes this an architecture answer, not a services quiz.

CloudFront & the edge — caching where the users are

      
advanced → cache keys, invalidation & signed access

    

  

  

    
CloudFront parks copies of your responses in ~600 locations near users: static assets obviously, but also API responses (short TTLs), and even dynamic content benefits from edge connection termination (TLS handshakes complete near the user; the long haul rides AWS's backbone). The engineering lives in two decisions. The cache key: which parts of the request (path, which headers, which query params, cookies?) define "the same response" — too broad a key and users see each other's content (the cache-poisoning family of bugs); too narrow and your hit ratio evaporates (every UTM param a fresh miss). And invalidation strategy: paths can be invalidated (slow-ish, costs per path), but the grown-up pattern is immutable filenames — app.3f9c2.js cached forever, deploys just reference new names (the frontend course's asset story, seen from the server side).

    

      

        
cache-key-design.js — run it (hit ratios under three key choices)

        
// 1000 requests for /api/cats: 3 real variants (en/de/fr via Accept-Language),
// but every request carries a unique utm_source query param
function simulate(keyOf) {
  const cache = new Map();
  let hits = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < 1000; i++) {
    const req = {
      path: '/api/cats',
      lang: ['en', 'de', 'fr'][i % 3],
      query: `utm_source=campaign${i}`,            // unique every time!
      user: `u${i % 40}`,
    };
    const key = keyOf(req);
    if (cache.has(key)) hits++;
    else cache.set(key, `response-${req.lang}`);
  }
  return { hits: (hits / 10).toFixed(1) + '%', variants: cache.size };
}

console.log('key = path only:                 ', JSON.stringify(simulate(r => r.path)),
  ' ← 99% hits BUT German users get English (wrong-content bug)');
console.log('key = path+lang (correct):       ', JSON.stringify(simulate(r => r.path + r.lang)));
console.log('key = path+lang+query (naive):   ', JSON.stringify(simulate(r => r.path + r.lang + r.query)),
  ' ← utm params nuked the cache');
console.log('key = path+lang+user:            ', JSON.stringify(simulate(r => r.path + r.lang + r.user)),
  ' ← per-user keys: why personalized responses do not edge-cache');
console.log('\nthe craft: include EXACTLY what changes the response — nothing else.');

      

      
same traffic, four hit ratios — the key definition IS the caching strategy

      

    

    

      
// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

      
Paid course videos live in S3. Users must access them only while subscribed, via CDN. The pattern?

      

        
        
        
        
      

      
Why: obscurity (A) is one shared link from collapse; proxying (C) is the §08 bytes-through-my-server mistake with video-sized consequences; raw S3 presigns (D) work but forfeit edge caching and per-region performance for your biggest objects. Signed URLs at the CDN put the auth check at the same place as the cache — OAC keeps S3 sealed except to CloudFront. Layered: bucket private, edge authorized, links expiring.

Infrastructure as code — desired state, diffed

      
advanced → why clicking is a liability, and what plan/apply really do

    

  

  

    
Console-clicked infrastructure has three fatal properties: it's unreproducible (staging ≠ prod in ways nobody can list), unreviewable (no diff, no PR, no "who added the 0.0.0.0/0 ingress?"), and undocumented by construction. IaC (Terraform/OpenTofu, CloudFormation, CDK, Pulumi) inverts it: you declare desired state in versioned files; the tool reads actual state, computes a diff, and shows the plan — create this, change that, destroy this — before touching anything. Review the plan like code review, because it is: the plan is the PR diff for your infrastructure.

    
Drift is the enemy the workflow guards against: someone hotfixes in the console, reality diverges from the files, and the next apply either reverts their fix or the files lie forever. The discipline: the files are the truth, console changes are incidents.

    

      

        
mini-plan.js — run it (desired vs actual → a plan)

        
const desired = {
  'sg.app':      { ingress: '3000 from alb-sg' },
  'db.main':     { size: 'db.r6g.large', multiAZ: true },
  's3.assets':   { versioning: true },
  'cdn.main':    { origin: 's3.assets' },
};
const actual = {
  'sg.app':      { ingress: '3000 from 0.0.0.0/0' },   // 3am console "fix" — drift!
  'db.main':     { size: 'db.r6g.large', multiAZ: true },
  's3.assets':   { versioning: false },                 // never enabled
  'legacy.ec2':  { size: 't2.micro' },                  // in the account, not in code
};

console.log('terraform plan:\n');
for (const key of new Set([...Object.keys(desired), ...Object.keys(actual)])) {
  const d = desired[key], a = actual[key];
  if (d && !a) console.log(`  + create  ${key}  ${JSON.stringify(d)}`);
  else if (!d && a) console.log(`  - DESTROY ${key}  ← unmanaged resource: import it or lose it`);
  else if (JSON.stringify(d) !== JSON.stringify(a))
    console.log(`  ~ change  ${key}  ${JSON.stringify(a)} → ${JSON.stringify(d)}`);
  else console.log(`    ok      ${key}`);
}
console.log('\ntwo findings tell two stories:');
console.log('· sg.app drift: someone opened the app to the world in the console — the plan CAUGHT it');
console.log('· the DESTROY line: why you read plans before applying. Always. Every time.');

      

      
the security-group drift is the whole argument for IaC in one line

      

    

    

      
// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

      
During an incident, an engineer widened a security group in the console. Post-incident, what's the correct reconciliation?

      

        
        
        
        
      

      
Why: unreconciled drift is a time bomb with two triggers — the next routine apply silently reverts a "fix" someone depends on, or the files permanently lie about your security posture (an un-reviewed 0.0.0.0/0 living in prod). Console-in-emergency is fine; unrecorded afterward is the sin. Option C corrupts the tool's memory of reality; option D documents the lie instead of resolving it. "Drift is an open incident until reconciled" is the operating principle.

Running it — knowing before the users tweet

      
intermediate → logs, metrics, alarms, and the deploy loop

    

  

  

    
CloudWatch is three tools sharing a name. Logs: every container's stdout (the node module's structured JSON) lands in log groups — searchable with Logs Insights; request ids make one user's journey traceable. Metrics: numbers over time — the free ones (CPU, ALB 5xx rate, target response time, queue depth, DB connections) plus your custom RED metrics per route. Alarms: thresholds on metrics that page you — and the discipline is alarming on symptoms users feel (5xx rate, p99 latency, queue age) rather than causes (CPU%), because causes without symptoms are just weather.

    
The loop that makes it real: deploy (rolling, health-checked) → watch the release-annotated dashboards → alarm fires → logs filtered by request id → fix or roll back (the previous task definition is one click). Health checks split exactly as in the node module: liveness ("restart me") vs readiness ("don't route to me") — ALBs act on the second; conflating them causes the restart-storm-during-DB-blip incident everyone has once.

    

      
// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

      
Which alarm set is right for the stage-2 café app — pick the SYMPTOM-first set?

      

        
        
        
        
      

      
Why: a CPU spike serving fast responses needs no human; a 5xx spike at 2% CPU absolutely does. Symptom alarms have near-zero false positives (users ARE hurting) and cause metrics stay on dashboards for diagnosis, not paging. "Queue oldest-message age" over "queue length" is the connoisseur pick — length can be high and healthy; age growing means consumers are losing. Alert fatigue from cause-alarms is why on-call rotations burn out — saying that shows you've lived it.

  • Auto-scaling policies read like alarms too: target-tracking on CPU or ALB requests-per-target for the API; queue depth for workers — "scale on the same signals you'd page on, one notch earlier".

The bill — cost as an architecture property

      
advanced → the four line items and the classic surprises

    

  

  

    
AWS bills for four things, roughly in this order for a typical app: compute time (EC2/Fargate hours, Lambda GB-seconds), database (instance hours + storage + Multi-AZ doubling), data transfer OUT (the sneaky one — into AWS is free, out to the internet costs, cross-AZ costs both ways), and storage (cheap until you never delete anything). The classic surprises are all transfer: NAT gateway hourly + per-GB fees for private-subnet internet access, cross-AZ chatter between services, and un-cached images served from S3 instead of CloudFront.

    
Cost discipline that fits in one paragraph: tag everything (know which feature spends), budgets + billing alarms from day one (the free insurance nobody buys), right-size after measuring (the t3.large at 4% CPU), Savings Plans once steady-state is known (~30–40% off compute you'll run anyway), lifecycle-expire old S3 data and logs, and put CloudFront in front of anything users download twice. And the architecture-level truth: the cheapest request is the one a cache absorbed — CloudFront and Redis are cost tools as much as latency tools.

    

      
// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

      
The bill doubled this month. Traffic didn't. The three most likely suspects, in order?

      

        
        
        
        
      

      
Why: method first — Cost Explorer's group-by-service + the tag report turns a mystery into a diff; the trio above covers most real doublings. Compromise (option 3) is real but announces itself as GPU instances in regions you've never used — worth one glance, not panic. Multi-AZ doubling is visible the month you enable it, not randomly. "Cost is observable like latency — measure, don't speculate" is the transferable sentence.

    

    

      
LEVEL UP — what interviewers listen for

      
    
        
  • Lambda's economics in one line: free at zero, brilliant at spiky, and at sustained high throughput a steady Fargate service is often cheaper — do the arithmetic, not the ideology.
    • 
        
  • "We put a billing alarm at 2× expected on day one" is the cheapest sentence that sounds like scar tissue — because it always is.
    • 
        
  • The interview close: cost, latency, and resilience are the same three-way trade — every cache, replica, and AZ is a position in it. Frame answers that way and infra questions become design questions.
