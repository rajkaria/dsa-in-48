The interview kit. Write in it.

Offers are won in the technical rounds and lost everywhere else: the fumbled "tell me about yourself", the story you remembered in the elevator down, the negotiation that never happened. This module is a workbook — the worksheets save as you type (in this browser, nowhere else), so it becomes YOUR kit, not another article you read once.

It pairs with Behavioral in 48 (the full behavioral course); this is the everything-else: pitch, logistics, questions, money, and the planner that schedules the entire track. Fill it in over a week of coffees. ☕🐈

// before the loop — the paper trail
01

The story bank — six stories cover every question

the workbook's heart: write them once, deploy forever

Behavioral rounds ask forty different questions that map to about six stories. Write each as STAR+: Situation (one sentence of context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what YOU did — "I", not "we", with technical meat), Result (numbers where possible), and the +: what you'd do differently — the reflection that separates senior answers. Two to four sentences per letter; you're writing retrieval cues, not essays.

PROFESSOR WHISKERS EXPLAINSA cat doesn't improvise the pounce — she rehearses it on every sock in the house, so when the real mouse appears the movement is already in her body. Write the stories now, say them aloud twice, and in the room you'll be remembering, not composing. Composing under adrenaline is how good engineers give bad interviews.

LEVEL UP
  • Tag each story with the questions it answers ("failure" also answers "risk", "feedback", "wrong decision") — one bank, forty questions. The full mapping lives in Behavioral in 48 · the story bank.
  • Say each story aloud once after writing it — sentences that read fine often speak clunky. Edit for the mouth, not the eye.

02

The 90-second pitch — "tell me about yourself", solved

the question you WILL be asked, scripted on purpose

Every loop opens with it, and it sets the interviewer's prior for everything after. The shape that works: present → proof → pivot. Present: one sentence of who you are professionally ("full-stack engineer, N years, React/Node/Postgres, currently building X at Y"). Proof: two concrete highlights with numbers — chosen to match THIS job's needs. Pivot: why this role, specifically, stated with energy ("which is why this team's work on Z is exactly where I want to go deeper"). Ninety seconds, then stop — a clean stop invites the next question; trailing off invites doubt.

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

Where do most "tell me about yourself" answers actually fail?

Why: the question is an editing test disguised as small talk — they have your résumé; what they're learning is what YOU think matters. Selection (two highlights aimed at their needs) + the pivot + the stop is the whole craft. Rehearse it aloud five times; it's the highest-ROI ninety seconds in the entire prep.

03

The resume pass — six seconds, then the robot

before the loop → the document that decides if there IS a loop

Two readers, neither of them careful: an ATS keyword-matcher, then a human giving it six seconds. Format for both at once: one page (two if 10+ years), no columns/graphics/photos (ATS parsers shred them), reverse-chronological, and the skills the JD names appearing verbatim where true — the robot matches strings, not synonyms. The human's six seconds land on: current title, company, first bullet of the top role. Those four things ARE the first impression; engineer them.

The bullet formula that survives both readers: strong verb + what you built + measurable outcome — "Built idempotent payment-webhook processing (Node, SQS), cutting duplicate charges to zero across 40k orders/mo." Not responsibilities ("responsible for backend services") — receipts. Numbers anchor credibility even when approximate: latency shaved, revenue touched, users served, incidents prevented. One rewrite drill below; the standard is "could a stranger verify this in the interview by asking one follow-up?" — because a good interviewer will.

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

JD says "experience with Kubernetes". You've run ECS for three years, touched k8s in a side project. The resume move?

Why: the resume's claims get load-tested live — "tell me about your k8s setup" against an inflated bullet is how strong loops die in round one. Truthful scoping passes the keyword filter AND converts the follow-up into an honest strength ("deep on ECS, conversant in k8s, orchestration concepts transfer"). Omitting (C) fails the robot for no reason. The principle generalizes: every line is a question you're inviting; only invite the ones you want.

LEVEL UP — what actually moves the needle
  • Tailoring is 15 minutes, not a rewrite: reorder bullets so the JD-relevant ones lead, and mirror the JD's vocabulary where true.
  • LinkedIn is the inbound half: same headline formula, "open to work" (recruiters-only mode exists), and the About section is your 90-second pitch (§02) in writing.
  • Projects section earns its space only with receipts (users, stars, "this page you're reading") — otherwise cut for whitespace; recruiters read density as desperation.
  • The file itself: FirstLast_Role.pdf, text-selectable (parse-test it by copy-pasting into a text editor — what garbles for you garbles for the ATS).

04

The pipeline — referrals, recruiters & parallel timing

before the loop → job search as a funnel you operate, not a lottery you enter

Cold applications convert at ~1-2%; referrals at 10x that — so the search is a pipeline problem: top of funnel (referrals first: former colleagues, alumni, meetup contacts — the ask is small and specific: "would you be comfortable referring me for [exact role]? two-line blurb attached"), middle (recruiter screens — the 90-second pitch's home turf), bottom (loops). Operate it with a tracker (worksheet below): company, stage, next action, dates — because the search's real killer is dropped threads, not rejections.

The strategic layer is timing: offers expire, so loops must land together. Sequence: warm-up loops first (companies you're lukewarm on — free mock interviews with real stakes), targets second, and batch the applications so on-sites cluster inside two weeks. One live offer is the strongest possible accelerant for every other pipeline ("I have an offer with a deadline" moves recruiters like nothing else) — and that only works if the funnel was parallel, not serial.

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

Dream-company recruiter calls TODAY, but you haven't interviewed anywhere in two years. Take the loop now?

Why: scheduling is soft — recruiters juggle calendars for weeks as a matter of course, and "excited, need three weeks" costs nothing. Burning the real target as a warm-up (D) is backwards sequencing (cooldown periods are 6-12 months); infinite delay (C) is fear wearing a plan. The meta-lesson is that the funnel has controls — dates are negotiable, order is choosable — and operators use them.

LEVEL UP — what actually moves the needle
  • The referral ask that works: specific role + link + two-line blurb + explicit out ("totally fine to say no"). You're drafting their internal message for them.
  • Follow-up cadence: one polite nudge after 7 quiet days, then move on — the tracker's "next action" column exists so silence never dangles.
  • Rejections are data: screen-stage rejections → resume problem (§03); post-loop → drill the cracked round (the debrief log, §05). Route the signal, skip the grief.
  • Keep the pipeline warm even when happy: a coffee chat per month costs nothing and turns the next search from cold-start into warm-start. (The planner's "ongoing" column, socialized.)

// the loop — rehearsal & rubrics
05

Mock-loop run sheets — rehearse the whole day

simulate the loop before the loop simulates you

A real loop is 3–5 rounds back to back — and stamina, context-switching, and recovery-from-a-bad-round are trainable, but only by simulating the day. The run sheet: Round 1 (45'): a timed DSA problem, out loud, from DSA in 48's drills. Break (10') — practice the reset ritual: water, walk, three breaths, next round is a new interviewer who knows nothing about the last one. Round 2 (45'): a system design prompt (module §07's five, or frontend via module 12). Round 3 (45'): behavioral — four questions from Behavioral in 48, answered from your §01 bank. Debrief (15'): the worksheet below, immediately, while it's raw.

Do one full loop-day per week of prep. The first one is humbling; the third one is transformative. Recruit a friend for one round if you can — even a non-engineer catches "you said um for thirty seconds" truths.

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

You bomb round 2 of a real loop. What does the evidence on interview outcomes say to do?

Why: debriefs average independent signals — one bad round with strong others is a hire at most companies; two rounds sacrificed to one bad round is not. The reset ritual you practiced in mock loops is FOR this exact moment. (And round 3's interviewer genuinely doesn't know — don't tell them.) This is why the run sheet trains the break as seriously as the rounds.

Questions, both ways — the banks and the reversal

what they'll ask, and the questions YOU ask

Their side is covered across the series — the rapid-fire banks in Full-Stack (56), Frontend SD (48), and Behavioral (40) total ~150 real questions with answers. The kit's job is the routing table: screens → fullstack bank; frontend loops → frontend bank + module 12; senior loops → behavioral bank + your §01 stories. 07

The system-design rubric — what the grader's sheet actually says

the loop → score yourself the way they score you

Design interviewers file structured feedback, and the axes barely vary across companies: problem navigation (did you scope requirements and drive, or wait to be steered?), quantitative grounding (the envelope — numbers before architecture), breadth (a complete skeleton: client → edge → service → data → async), depth on demand (two layers deeper wherever probed — this is where the sd/fesd modules' advanced bands pay), tradeoff articulation (every choice priced against its alternative), and seniority signals (failure modes volunteered, operability mentioned, right-sized solutions — the "I would NOT build" sentences). Juniors present architectures; seniors narrate decisions — same boxes, different soundtrack, and the rubric only hears the soundtrack.

Self-scoring after every mock (worksheet below) converts the rubric from trivia into a training loop: score 1-5 per axis, drill the lowest axis before the next mock, re-score. Two mocks of targeted repair beat ten of general rehearsal.

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

Two candidates draw identical feed architectures. A explains as: "posts go to the queue, then fan out to feed lists in Redis." B: "reads outnumber writes 100:1 here, so I precompute feeds at write time — the cost is write amplification for big accounts, which I'd cap with a hybrid pull for celebrities." Who levels higher, and which axes?

Why: the drawing is maybe a third of the grade; the rubric's other axes live entirely in the narration. B's three sentences hit three axes A left blank — with the same design. This is why rubric-aware practice compounds: you're not learning more architecture, you're learning to emit the evidence the sheet is listening for. (And D's cynicism inverts: tricks become judgment precisely when you attach the reasoning.)

LEVEL UP — what the graders talk about after
  • The strongest single habit: close every component with its failure story unprompted — "if Redis dies here, we degrade to…" hits depth + seniority in one breath (the sd module's closing ritual, now explained by WHY it works).
  • Level calibration in one line: mid = completes the design when steered; senior = drives and prices tradeoffs; staff = questions the requirements and right-sizes ("do we need multi-region for this?").
  • Silence reads as absence: unsaid reasoning scores zero — narrate even the obvious ("SQL here, nothing exotic — access patterns are relational and scale is modest").
  • Ask your mock partner to grade THE AXES, not vibes — "was I quantitative?" gets actionable answers where "how did I do?" gets kindness.

08

Take-homes — scope like a senior, README like a marketer

the loop → the assignment is "how do you work?", answer THAT

A take-home isn't graded on features shipped — it's a proxy for "what's this person like as a colleague?" The winning shape: a small core, executed excellently — working happy path, honest error handling on the paths that matter, a few well-chosen tests (the judgment of WHICH things you tested is itself graded), clean naming, and zero half-finished stretch goals. Six polished hours beat sixteen sprawling ones; reviewers smell scope-creep as poor judgment, not ambition.

The README is half the grade: how to run it in two commands, what you built, and — the section that levels you — decisions and tradeoffs: "SQLite over Postgres for reviewer-runs-it-in-one-command; the repository layer makes swapping trivial. Skipped auth per the brief; here's where it would slot. With another day: X, then Y." That paragraph does what the design-round soundtrack does (§07): it converts your code from an artifact into a narrated decision. Timebox honestly (if it says 4 hours, spend ≤6 and say so), and make it run first try — a reviewer who can't npm i && npm start stops reading.

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

Four hours into a "build a URL shortener API" take-home, you have a clean core. Two remaining hours: which spend wins?

Why: reviewers run the happy path, poke one error, read the tests, read the README — your two hours should land exactly where their twenty minutes go. Three half-features (A) read as "starts things"; an unrequested frontend (C) reads as "ignores briefs". Deploy/CI (D) is the one defensible alternative if the role is infra-flavored — knowing the audience decides the tiebreak, which is the actual skill under test.

LEVEL UP — what reviewers notice in the first five minutes
  • Commit history tells your story too: small, described commits read as method; one "final" commit reads as chaos survived.
  • Seed data + a .env.example + an http file/curl block = a reviewer productive in 90 seconds. Friction is failure.
  • If you exceed the timebox, say so in the README ("brief said 4h; I spent 6 — the extra went to tests") — honesty about time is itself a signal.
  • The follow-up call walks YOUR code — re-read it the morning of, and be ready to extend it live ("add expiring links") in your own architecture. Home-turf advantage only helps if you still know the turf.

// after the loop — money & the decision
09

The offer — the ten minutes worth lakhs

negotiation basics nobody teaches engineers

The rules that survive contact with reality. 1 — Never give the first number. "What are your expectations?" → "I'd rather understand the full scope first — what's the band for this level?" (Bands exist. Recruiters can share them. In many places they legally must.) 2 — The offer call is for gratitude, not decisions: "I'm excited — let me review the full picture and come back Thursday." Enthusiasm + delay is the entire script. 3 — Negotiate total comp, not base: base, bonus, equity (with its vesting and refresh story), joining bonus — the joining bonus is the easiest yes in the building. 4 — Competing timelines are leverage, politely: "I have another loop concluding next week" is a fact, not a threat, and it moves numbers. 5 — Every ask is easier with a reason attached — the market data point, the competing number, the level mismatch — and 'the band's midpoint given my N years with this exact stack' is a reason.

And the one that changes careers more than any single number: negotiate the level, not just the money — a level too low compounds for years of raises and promotions. If the offer's level reads below your loop performance, that's the conversation to have first. The deep dive — leveling, counter-scripts, the full decision framework — is Behavioral in 48 · negotiation; this section is the card you re-read before the call.

10

Comp literacy — reading an offer like a balance sheet

after the loop → base, bonus, equity: what each is actually worth

Section 09 armed your numbers; this section makes sure you're pricing the right thing. Total comp = base + bonus + equity/year, and the three are different animals: base is certain and compounds (raises/bonuses percent off it); bonus is "target %" — ask what the team actually hit the last two years; equity is maybe-money whose value depends on what the company is. Public company RSUs: shares × price, real money on a vesting schedule (4 years, often a 1-year cliff — leave at month 11, get nothing). Startup options: a strike price to buy shares that are illiquid until an exit — price them honestly by asking: latest 409A vs strike (the spread is today's paper value), preferred vs common (investors exit first), exercise window if you leave (90 days to pay cash for paper is the classic trap). The one-line calibration: RSUs at FAANG ≈ salary; options at a seed startup ≈ a lottery ticket you should value near zero when comparing against certain money.

The scripts that do the work: on being asked expectations first — "I'd like to understand the full picture before naming numbers; what's the range for this level?" (they have a range; anchoring first against yourself is the classic loss). On the offer — gratitude + "I'd like a few days" (never accept live), then the counter with a reason attached: "Company B is at X total / levels.fyi shows Y for this level — can we close the gap on equity or base?" Recruiters expect the counter; the first number is almost never the last.

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

Offer A: ₹45L base + ₹15L/yr public-company RSUs. Offer B: ₹48L base + options "worth ₹30L/yr" at a Series-B startup. How do you compare them?

Why: "worth ₹30L/yr" is the company's own projection of its own success — RSUs you can sell on vest day are a different asset class from options behind a preference stack and an exit that may never come. The adult version isn't cynicism (C) — startups mint real fortunes — it's informed risk: know the four numbers, decide your lottery budget, and negotiate B's base up precisely because its equity is uncertain. That last move — "your equity is risky, so certain comp must carry more" — is the negotiation sentence this section exists to teach.

LEVEL UP — the details that change outcomes
  • Level beats percent: a level bump moves every future number; if the offer is maxed for the level, negotiate the level (or the review date: "L+1 review at 6 months, in writing").
  • Sign-on bonuses are the gap-closer companies prefer (one-time, doesn't compound) — take it knowingly, and check the clawback period.
  • Ask about refreshers: initial grants deplete after year 4 (the "vesting cliff" of year-5 comp) — companies with strong refresh cultures compound; ones without quietly cut your pay in year five.
  • Everything in writing before resigning anywhere — verbal comp promises are weather.

11

The decision matrix — choosing when both offers are good

after the loop → decide with your weights, not your recency bias

Two good offers is a champagne problem that still ruins sleep, because comp is the easiest axis to compare and rarely the one that decides your next two years. The matrix (worksheet below): list YOUR axes — growth (who will you learn from? seniors above you or a title with no mentors?), scope (what will you own?), manager (the interview was them interviewing too — what did you observe?), team health (ask THEM: on-call load, last incident, attrition), comp (from §10, priced honestly), stability/risk appetite, commute/remote, mission-if-you-care. Weight the axes first (before scoring! — weights chosen after peeking are rationalizations), score each offer 1-5, multiply, sum.

Then the honest part: the matrix's job is usually to reveal your decision, not make it — if the total says A and your stomach sinks, the weights were wrong; fix them and notice which weight you moved (that's the real priority talking). Timing collisions get the §04 playbook: ask A for a week citing an in-flight process ("I want to decide with complete information — deadlines this tight usually flex"), tell B a decision date exists. Exploding offers that truly won't flex are themselves data about the company.

// quiz — first pick is the one that counts

Offer A explodes Friday; dream-company B's onsite is next Wednesday. The strongest play?

Why: both deadlines are softer than they present — A's recruiter would rather extend than lose a yes, and B's would rather expedite than lose you to a deadline; you're the only party not using the phone. Accept-then-renege (A) burns a network you'll meet again (do it only with real justification and open eyes — it happens, it costs). The worksheet's role: if A-won't-budge arrives, you decide A against your weights, not against a fantasy of B — "would I take A if B didn't exist?" is the question that cuts through.

LEVEL UP — the questions that out-predict the matrix
  • Ask every prospective manager: "what did the last person in this role do next?" — trajectory of predecessors beats any pitch.
  • Attrition question, phrased answerable: "how many of the team were here a year ago?" — hesitation IS the answer.
  • The two-years test: "which offer makes the BEST possible version of 2028-me?" — comp differences fade; compounding skill/scope gaps don't.
  • Decline the loser warmly and specifically — "chose more mentorship-heavy" — recruiters remember gracious no's; the pipeline (§04) is forever.

13

Day-before & day-of — the rituals

boring on purpose; boring is what works under adrenaline

Day before: nothing new — re-reads only (the matching cheat tables, your story bank, your pitch). One full RADIO or DSA pattern pass out loud, timed, then STOP by early evening. Prep the logistics like a flight: link tested, camera framed, water filled, notes page open (one page max), charger in the wall. Sleep is a performance drug; take it seriously.

Day of: warm up the voice and the brain 30 minutes before — explain one favorite concept aloud to the wall (or the cat, who has heard the event loop explanation eleven times and remains unimpressed). In the room: ask the clarifying question FIRST even when you think you understand (it buys ten seconds of calm and occasionally saves the round), narrate always, and when you don't know — the honest redirect from the courses' playbooks, never the bluff. Between rounds: the §03 reset ritual. After the loop: the debrief worksheet within an hour, every question you remember — three loops in, you've seen most of the deck.

Last thing, and it's not a platitude: you've shipped real systems for years. The interviews test recall and narration of things you already do — which is exactly what this track trained. Walk in like someone reviewing their own work, because that's what this is. 🐾

14

The reasoning gym — nine questions, three tiers

all levels → transfer, not recall: every question is an inference

Thirteen sections built the kit; this one trains the judgment that swings it. Warm-up isolates one call against its favorite bad instincts — every wrong option is something a smart, tired candidate actually says. Apply hands you a live situation and makes you trace it to a decision; stretch chains sections the way real weeks do — timing against money, prep hours against rubrics. First pick is the recorded one, and the why under each answer is the actual lesson — read it even when you're right.

// warm-up 1 of 3 — spot the strongest failure answer

The round asks: "tell me about a time you failed." Four candidates, four answers — which is strongest?

Why: the question is scored on the + in STAR+ (§01): a real failure, owned in the first person, with the lesson operationalized into something that outlived the incident. The perfectionism answer is a dodge wearing a costume — interviewers have heard it enough to deduct on sight. The requirements answer shifts blame, which reads as "will do the same here." The prod-outage answer has the honesty but ends on the damage — feelings aren't the +, the checklist is. Strongest ≠ the most impressive disaster; strongest = the clearest ownership-to-system pipeline.

// warm-up 2 of 3 — who names the first number

Minute four of the first recruiter screen: "so — what are your salary expectations?" The strongest reply?

Why: §09 rule 1 — never give the first number. Current-comp-plus-20% negotiates against yourself, politely: their band may sit far above your arithmetic, and your stated number becomes the ceiling. Blind-anchoring high fails the other way — anchoring works when you know the range, and at minute four you don't; you can price yourself out of a process nobody's invested in yet. Total silence overcorrects: the screen is exactly when a band mismatch should surface, before either side spends a month. Deflect-to-band gets you the information while giving none.

// warm-up 3 of 3 — grade your own questions

Final five minutes of a technical round: "any questions for me?" Which is the strongest use of the slot?

Why: the slot is graded as the last impression (§06): "no questions" reads as no curiosity, not efficiency. The what-does-the-company-do question proves the opposite of curiosity — that you didn't research. The band question is the right question in the wrong channel: money routes to the recruiter (§09); an engineer can't answer it, so the slot burns for nothing. The postmortem question is §06's role's-reality tier — it demonstrates operating instincts, produces an answer nobody rehearsed, and how a team talks about failure tells you whether §01's ownership culture is how they actually live.

// apply 1 of 3 — operate the pipeline clock

Monday morning. Your tracker: warm-up company A — onsite went well, offer expected Friday. Target B — recruiter replied last Thursday, screen not yet booked. Target C — onsite in three weeks. Today's highest-leverage move?

Why: two §04 mechanisms have to fire together — offers expire, so loops only help each other if they land together, and a live offer is the accelerant that makes recruiters expedite. Both point the same way: act before Friday, because "an offer is coming" compresses B's timeline for free. Waiting is the serial funnel — it spends A's expiry window doing nothing. Declining A throws away the exact artifact the warm-up loop exists to produce. Pushing C out un-clusters the loops you're trying to cluster. Operators touch the controls: dates are negotiable in both directions.

// apply 2 of 3 — route feedback to rubric axes

Your mock partner's notes from a design round: "full skeleton, client through async workers; handled both deep probes when I pushed; never wrote down a single number; presented every choice as the obvious one — no alternative even mentioned." Which axes get this week's drill?

Why: §07's self-scoring is a routing exercise — each observation maps to an axis, and the drill goes to the lowest one. Breadth and depth are the two axes these notes passed (full skeleton, probes survived); drilling them is practicing your serve because you like your serve. Navigation isn't in the evidence at all, and no axis officially outweighs the rest — every blank one costs. "None" is the halo effect: one strong axis vouching for five silent ones, which is precisely the bias the §07 worksheet exists to break.

// apply 3 of 3 — trace the vesting calendar

Your grant: ₹48L of public-company RSUs over 4 years — 1-year cliff, quarterly vesting after that. Fourteen months in, a better offer lands and you resign. How much equity have you actually banked?

Why: vesting is chunky, not continuous — nothing until month 12, then 25% (₹12L) in one drop, then ₹3L per quarter at months 15, 18, 21… At month 14 you hold exactly the cliff. ₹14L is the monthly-accrual instinct — some schedules do vest monthly after the cliff, but this one said quarterly, and reading the schedule you actually have is the skill (§10). ₹0 confuses unvested (forfeited, yes) with vested (banked, yours). The career move hiding in the arithmetic: resignation dates are choosable — month 15 versus month 14 is ₹3L for one Tuesday. Never resign without the vesting calendar open.

// stretch 1 of 3 — find the false claim

Three of these claims come straight out of the kit. Which one is FALSE?

Why: the false one inverts §09 rule 2 — the offer call is for gratitude and a delay ("I'm excited — let me review and come back Thursday"), because negotiating live, on adrenaline, without your §09 numbers in front of you is playing their game on their turf, and the number does not shrink because you slept on it. The other three are load-bearing: independent debriefs are why §05's reset ritual works, narration-carries-the-grade is the entire §07 lesson, and won't-flex deadlines feeding the §11 matrix is how pressure becomes data. The trap is that the false claim sounds like decisiveness — the kit's word for deciding at the speed of someone else's script is "anchored."

// stretch 2 of 3 — allocate hours by marginal return

Ten days to a real onsite — DSA, system design, behavioral. Budget: one focused weekday hour plus a 4-hour weekend block ≈ 12h total. Your state: DSA sharp, never run a live design round, story bank empty. Which spend wins?

Why: allocate by marginal return, not comfort. The extra DSA hour buys almost nothing — that round was already yours — while the first design mock and the first written story are the cheapest points on the board (§01's whole bet is that six stories fit in two focused hours). Even thirds is the fairness heuristic aimed at a problem that isn't fair: your weakest round moves several times more per hour than your strongest. The reading plan breaks §12's compression rule — cut depth, never mocks — because §07's rubric grades performance, and watching performance trains spectating. And the full loop-day matters more than it looks: §05's claim is that stamina and recovery are separately trainable, and the onsite tests them either way.

// stretch 3 of 3 — read an offer's red flags

Series-B offer: base exactly at your walk-away number; options "worth ₹35L a year" by the company's own math; a 48-hour deadline; the recruiter declines to share strike price vs latest 409A; refreshers are "standard here, but we don't put that in writing." The correct read?

Why: each flag alone is survivable; together they're a pattern. §10's pricing questions — strike vs 409A, preference stack, exercise window — are the minimum information for valuing options, so a refusal makes the honest price "unknown, therefore ≈ zero against certain money." Verbal refreshers are §10's weather; the won't-flex deadline is §11's data. But auto-withdrawal overcorrects: red flags are inputs to the decision matrix, not verdicts — a base at your walk-away with the equity zeroed is, by your own §09 numbers, exactly the borderline case the matrix exists to decide. The move is always the same: convert pressure into information, in writing, then weigh.

LEVEL UP — how to read your score
  • Warm-up misses → reread the section each why names (§01, §09, §06). Every distractor up there is a classic instinct with a name — the pseudo-failure, the self-anchor, the polite non-question — and knowing the name is how you catch it leaving your own mouth.
  • Apply misses → adopt the tracing ritual: before choosing, write the state down — pipeline dates on one line, vesting tranches on a timeline, partner-notes mapped axis by axis against §07. The gym keeps its scenarios small enough to hold in your head; real ones aren't, and the write-it-down habit is the part that transfers.
  • Stretch misses → name the pairings and re-walk them: §04×§09 (timing IS leverage), §12×§05×§07 (compress depth, never the mocks), §10×§11 (price the equity, then weigh the matrix). No loop ever asks one section at a time — combining is the skill.
  • The transferable habit: argue against your first answer before committing — the distractors were built from first instincts. "Would I still pick this without the comforting phrase attached?" is the same test that catches inflated bullets in §03 and face-value equity in §10.
