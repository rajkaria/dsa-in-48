// a 48-hour syllabus for engineers with years of real work and zero packaged stories

The senior loop, cracked in one weekend.

Past five years of experience, the interview that decides your offer isn't the coding round — it's the one where a stranger asks "tell me about a time…" and you have fifteen seconds to pick the right story from years of shipping. Most strong engineers walk in with zero prepared material and improvise. That's the whole gap.

These two days turn your actual track record — the team you led, the migration you drove, the outage you owned — into a dozen tight, quantified stories and the judgment to deploy the right one, calibrated for senior roles at US product companies. Especially if you're punching a level above your title: seniority here is argued with evidence, not years.

// ground rules

How to run these two days

Day 1 you write; day 2 you speak. Every topic ends with drills that produce a real artifact — a story, a script, a list — that you'll reuse in every interview for years. Do them in a doc you keep.

01 · write, then say it out loud

A story that exists only in your head will collapse under pressure. Write every story, then record yourself telling it — the phone voice memo is the whole gym.

02 · numbers or it didn't happen

"Improved performance" is noise; "cut LCP 20–30% for 5M quarterly users" is signal. Dig up the real numbers this weekend while you can still find them.

03 · two minutes, hard stop

Every story gets told in 90–120 seconds, then you stop and let them dig. Rambling is the #1 senior-candidate failure mode. The timer is non-negotiable.

04 · "I", not "we"

Credit the team in one sentence, then narrate your decisions, your actions, your judgment. Interviewers can only hire the person in the room.

05 · only have one day?

Do the story bank, STAR, tell-me-about-yourself, and leadership stories. That's the material ~80% of behavioral questions draw from.

06 · the night before

Nothing new. Re-read your story bank once, re-record your 90-second pitch, check the question matcher, sleep. Freshness beats coverage.

day 1 · 09:00 · 60 minaim before you fire

What "senior" means in a US loop

You're not being graded on years — you're being graded against a leveling rubric you've never seen. Learn the rubric first and every story you write today aims at it.

YOU ALREADY KNOW THIS

You already operate at a level: you run sprint planning, own releases, unblock teammates, and answer for outcomes — you've just never had to label those behaviors in rubric language. This hour attaches the labels.

// the idea

  • Nearly every US product company grades on the same three axes: scope (how big a problem you own), autonomy (how much direction you need), and influence (whose work gets better because of you).
  • Mid-level: owns tasks, needs a defined problem. Senior: owns a problem end to end, defines the solution, lifts others. Staff: owns a problem space across teams. Interviewers listen for which one your stories describe.
  • The trap when punching above your title: telling task-level stories about leadership-level work. "I implemented X" is mid; "I noticed X was costing us Y, drove the fix across two teams, and it saved Z" is senior — often about the same work.
  • Years don't argue your level; altitude does. Five years leading releases, mentoring, and owning quality beats ten years of ticket-taking, but only if you narrate it at the right altitude.
  • Every interviewer files a written verdict against this rubric within minutes of the call ending. Your job is to hand them the evidence pre-sorted.
altitude-check.md
Same project, three altitudes. Spot the difference:

MID    "I was asked to improve our test coverage, so I added
        SonarQube rules and wrote tests until we hit the target."

SENIOR "Release regressions kept burning us. I made quality my
        problem: raised coverage 25%, cut critical issues 30%,
        and changed the definition-of-done so it stayed fixed."

STAFF  "Three teams had the same regression pattern. I got
        agreement on a shared quality bar, rolled it out org-wide,
        and regressions dropped across every team, not just mine."

The work is similar. The OWNERSHIP OF THE PROBLEM is the level.

day 1 · 10:00 · 90 minthe single highest-leverage hour

The story bank

Ten stories cover ninety percent of every behavioral question ever asked. Mine them once, this morning, and you'll never improvise under pressure again.

YOU ALREADY KNOW THIS

This is indexing. Right now your experience is a full-collection scan — every question triggers a linear search through years of memory, live, while someone watches. The story bank is the index you build once so every lookup is O(1).

// the idea

  • Walk your history job by job, project by project, and list moments — not achievements, moments: the day the release broke, the argument about the rewrite, the intern who couldn't ship, the deadline that moved.
  • From the list, pick ~10 stories that together cover the big eight categories: leadership · conflict · failure · ambiguity · influence · delivery under pressure · mentoring · initiative. One story can cover 2–3 categories from different angles — that's expected and good.
  • For each story capture five fields: situation (1 line) · your role · 2–3 decisions YOU made · outcome with a number · what you'd do differently. The last field is where senior candidates separate — juniors defend, seniors reflect.
  • Recency matters: at 7 years in, interviewers want mostly last-2-3-years material. One early-career story is fine; five is a red flag that you plateaued.
  • Spread stories across employers. All ten from one job reads as one-note; a story from a side project you own end to end (product decisions, users, iteration) is gold for product companies — it proves product sense no feature ticket can.
story-bank-template.md
# Story: The flaky-release quarter
covers: leadership, initiative, delivery        # categories
when:   2023–24, as team lead                   # recency check

SITUATION  Regressions kept escaping to prod; every release
           was a fire drill. Team of 5, fleet-safety product,
           2,000+ corporate customers.
MY ROLE    Team lead — quality was officially "everyone's job",
           which meant nobody's.
DECISIONS  1. Made coverage visible: SonarQube gate in CI.
           2. Refused to ship two releases until criticals hit 0.
           3. Re-planned sprints to include refactoring debt.
OUTCOME    Coverage +25%, critical issues −30%, releases boring.
LEARNED    Should have gotten team buy-in BEFORE adding the
           gate — first two weeks it was "the lead's rules",
           not "our bar". Rolled it back, re-proposed it in
           retro, and it stuck.

// spot it in the wild

  • "tell me about a time you…"index lookup — pick from the bank, never improvise
  • "do you have another example?"they're testing depth — this is why you need ~10, not 4
  • "something more recent?"recency check — lead with last-2-years stories by default

day 1 · 11:30 · 60 minthe delivery format

STAR, upgraded

Situation, Task, Action, Result — plus the two additions that mark a senior: a number in the Result and a Reflection after it. Two minutes, then stop talking.

YOU ALREADY KNOW THIS

STAR is a PR description: context, what changed and why, the measured result — written so a reviewer with zero context can follow. You've written hundreds. Now do it out loud, in two minutes.

// the idea

  • Situation — 20 seconds max. One sentence of context, one of stakes. The classic failure is spending a minute on backstory nobody needs.
  • Task — one sentence. What YOU were on the hook for. This is where "we" quietly erases you; keep it first person.
  • Action — the heart, ~60 seconds. Not what happened: what you decided, what you considered and rejected, and why. Decisions are the senior signal; activity is filler.
  • Result — with a number. Percentage, time saved, users affected, revenue, incidents avoided. If you truly have no metric, use scale ("2,000+ fleets") or a before/after contrast.
  • + Reflection — one sentence. "In hindsight I'd have done X sooner." Unprompted reflection reads as confidence, not weakness — and it preempts the "what would you do differently?" follow-up.
  • Then stop. Silence invites the follow-up, and follow-ups are where you win: they mean the interviewer is engaged and steering toward what they need.
two-minute-arc.md
0:00  SITUATION   "Our consumer platform served 5M users a
                   quarter, and the frontend was a monolith —
                   every team's release blocked every other's."
0:20  TASK        "As senior frontend, I owned unblocking it."
0:30  ACTION      "I proposed micro-frontends. Considered a
                   simple code-split first — rejected it because
                   the real cost was team coupling, not bundle
                   size. Rolled out one module as a pilot,
                   measured, then migrated the rest…"
1:30  RESULT      "Performance improved 20–30%, and teams
                   shipped independently for the first time."
1:45  REFLECTION  "I'd introduce the shared component library
                   FIRST next time — we duplicated UI for two
                   sprints before Storybook caught up."
2:00  — stop. Let them dig.

  • "so what was the outcome?"you buried the Result — restructure, lead with it next story
  • "what was YOUR part?"too much "we" — rewind and narrate your decisions

day 1 · 13:15 · 60 minasked in every single round

"Tell me about yourself" + the resume walkthrough

The only question you are guaranteed to get, in every round, by every interviewer. Ninety seconds, arc-shaped, ending at why you're in this specific room.

YOU ALREADY KNOW THIS

It's a README: what this project is, what problems it solves, why it exists — not the commit log. Nobody reads a README that starts "in 2015 I enrolled in…". Start at what it does now, gesture at how it got here, end at where it's going.

// the idea

  • Shape: now → how → why here. Who you are today (one line with your strongest credential), the 2–3 beats that built it (not every job — beats), and why this company/role is the logical next step. 90 seconds.
  • Lead with your headline, not your history: "I'm a team lead who's spent seven years on high-traffic web products — currently leading five engineers on fleet-safety software used by 2,000+ fleets" beats any chronology.
  • Every resume bullet is a deposition. If it says "improved performance 20–30%", you will be asked how it was measured, what the baseline was, and what you tried that didn't work. Tonight, annotate your own resume with those answers.
  • Career-gap / job-hop / short-stint questions get one calm factual sentence, forward-looking, zero apology — then bridge back to the work. Rehearse yours specifically; improvised versions ramble.
  • Have a 10-second version too ("seven years full-stack, lead a team of five, deep in React and Node") for recruiter screens and loop intros — you'll use it five times per company.
ninety-second-pitch.md
NOW    "I'm a technical team lead with 7 years building
        high-traffic web apps — right now I lead a team of 5
        shipping fleet-safety products for 2,000+ corporate
        fleets: sprint planning, code quality, releases."

HOW    "I got here through the frontend: at a consumer company
        serving 5M quarterly users I drove a micro-frontend
        migration that cut load times ~25%. Before that, four
        years shipping internal platforms for 100K+ employees.
        On the side I run a product of my own — a poker
        accounting app with 1,000+ weekly actives — where I own
        everything from product calls to the Postgres schema."

WHY    "I'm looking for a senior role at a product company
        where the engineering bar is high and I can own a
        problem end to end — which is exactly why this team."

— 85 seconds. Every clause is a hook they can pull.

day 1 · 14:15 · 90 minthe senior gate

Leadership & people stories

Conflict, underperformers, mentoring, influence without authority. These questions ARE the senior interview — technical rounds decide if you pass; these decide your level.

YOU ALREADY KNOW THIS

If you've led even a small team you have all of these stories: the heated code review, the engineer who kept missing sprint commitments, the designer you won over, the junior whose PRs went from red to green under your reviews. You've just never filed them as evidence.

// the idea — what each question is really measuring

  • Conflict ("disagreed with a colleague/manager"): can you disagree productively? The winning shape: steel-man their view → bring data → commit fully to whatever's decided, even when it's not your way. A story where you turned out wrong and said so is stronger than one where you won.
  • Underperformer: do you act or avoid? Show: noticed early → diagnosed privately (skill? clarity? life?) → concrete support with expectations → outcome either way. "I hoped it would improve" is the failing answer.
  • Mentoring: the senior gate. Name a real person (role, not name), what you actually did — pairing cadence, review style, stretch tasks — and where they ended up. Generic "I always help juniors" scores zero.
  • Influence without authority: the staff signal. How did you move people who don't report to you — other teams, PMs, skeptical architects? Levers: data, prototypes, allies, and making the right thing the easy thing.
  • Delegation & letting go (for lead roles): show you delegate outcomes, not tasks — and that you resisted grabbing the keyboard when it was slower to teach.
  • Never trash anyone in these stories. The other party is always reasonable-but-differently-incentivized; you look senior exactly to the degree they don't look stupid.
conflict-story-shape.md
Q: "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a decision."

WEAK   "My PM wanted to ship without tests. I pushed back and
        we shipped with tests."          # villain PM, no depth

STRONG "Our PM wanted to cut the testing window to hit a
        customer date — reasonable, the customer was at risk.
        I quantified the other side: our last two rushed
        releases each cost ~3 days of hotfixes. I proposed a
        middle path — ship the two low-risk modules on the
        date, hold the payments module one sprint. PM took it
        to the customer, who preferred it. Since then we scope
        release risk per-module by default. What I took away:
        deadlines are negotiable when you price the risk in
        THEIR currency — days and customer trust, not test
        coverage percentages."

  • "how did you grow your team?"mentoring evidence, specific human, measurable arc
  • "how do you get buy-in?"influence levers: data, pilot, allies — pick your best story

day 1 · 15:45 · 60 minwhere honesty wins

Failure, mistakes & feedback

"Tell me about a failure" is not a trap — it's a maturity check. The only wrong answers are a fake failure, a blamed teammate, or nothing learned.

YOU ALREADY KNOW THIS

It's a blameless postmortem, spoken. You've written these: what happened, the timeline, contributing causes, what we changed so it can't recur. Same document, first person, two minutes.

// the idea

  • Pick a real failure with real cost — a production incident you caused, a technical bet that didn't pay off, a deadline you blew. "I work too hard" and its cousins actively lose points at senior level.
  • But scope it: a failure you owned and survived, not a catastrophe that defines you. The story arc is competence → mistake → recovery → system change.
  • The senior tell is the last beat: what changed structurally. "I'm more careful now" is junior; "we added a canary stage and I now require rollback plans in every design doc" is senior.
  • Feedback questions ("hardest feedback you've received?") follow the same shape: real feedback that stung, taken seriously, with visible behavior change. Have one about your leadership style — at lead level, that's what they'll probe.
  • Deliver it matter-of-factly. Rehearsed calm about your failures reads as someone who's processed them; visible squirming reads as someone who hasn't.
failure-answer.md
"Early as a lead, I pushed a refactor of our auth flow and
 rolled it to all tenants at once — I was confident because
 staging was green. A tenant with a legacy SSO config got
 locked out for four hours; their whole fleet ops team was
 down. I owned the call with the customer, we rolled back,
 and I wrote the postmortem naming my decision as the cause.

 What changed: tenant-by-tenant rollout became our default
 for anything touching auth, and 'staging is green' stopped
 counting as evidence for config-dependent paths — we built
 a config-matrix test instead.

 It reset how I think about confidence: staging tells you
 your code works; only production tells you your ASSUMPTIONS
 work."

// spot it in the wild

  • "biggest mistake?" / "a project that failed?"postmortem story — cost, ownership, structural fix
  • "hardest feedback you've received?"real sting + visible change; have the leadership-style one ready
  • "what's your biggest weakness?"real, current, managed — name the mitigation system you use

day 1 · 16:45 · 60 minengineering maturity

Tradeoffs & engineering judgment

Tech debt vs features, deadlines vs quality, build vs buy, when NOT to refactor. These questions test whether you think like an owner or an artist.

YOU ALREADY KNOW THIS

Every sprint planning you've run is a tradeoff answer: you weighed debt against features against a date and shipped something defensible. You've also made the call from both sides — as the lead protecting quality and as the product owner of your own app cutting scope to ship. That dual view is exactly what they're probing.

// the idea

  • The universal senior answer shape: "it depends, and here's the framework I use" — then a real example where you applied it. Never a dogma ("always test everything"), never a shrug ("whatever the business wants").
  • Tech debt: frame it in product terms — debt is fine when it's a conscious loan with a payback trigger ("we hardcoded the config; the trigger to fix it is the third customer who needs it different"). Uncontrolled interest is what you refuse.
  • Quality vs deadline: price the risk in business currency (hotfix days, churn, trust), offer a scoped middle path, and let the owner of the outcome make the informed call. You're not the quality police; you're the risk quantifier.
  • Perfect vs shipped: have one story where you shipped something imperfect on purpose and it was right, and one where you held the line and it was right. Judgment is knowing which is which.
  • Ambiguity questions ("vague requirements, what do you do?"): show your loop — prototype, shrink the unknown, timebox, decide with 70% information. Waiting for certainty is the mid-level answer.
tradeoff-frame.md
Q: "How do you decide when tech debt is worth paying down?"

FRAME  "Debt has an interest rate. I look at what it costs
        per sprint — bug rate, onboarding time, how often it
        blocks a feature. Debt with near-zero interest can sit
        forever; debt we pay interest on every sprint gets a
        line in planning like any feature."

STORY  "On my current team, our test suite was the debt: 40
        minutes, flaky, so people merged around it. I costed
        it — roughly a day per engineer per week — which made
        the argument for me. Two sprints of investment cut it
        to 12 minutes, and merge-arounds stopped."

BOTH SIDES  "On my own product I run the other direction —
        I ship with known debt constantly, because with 1,000
        users the interest is near zero. Context sets the rate."

  • "a time you had incomplete requirements"ambiguity loop: prototype, timebox, decide at 70%
  • "would you rewrite or refactor?"the boring answer (incremental) with the one exception you'd rewrite

day 2 · 09:00 · 60 minsame stories, different rubric

Decode the company

Amazon grades against 16 named principles. Meta probes speed and impact. Startups probe scrappiness. Same story bank — you just change which beat you emphasize.

YOU ALREADY KNOW THIS

It's responsive design: one component, media queries per viewport. Your conflict story renders differently at Amazon (which principle did you defend?) than at a 40-person startup (how fast did you move on?) — same DOM, different breakpoints.

// the idea

  • Amazon is the extreme case: every behavioral question maps to a named Leadership Principle (Ownership, Bias for Action, Dive Deep, Deliver Results, Earn Trust…). Read all 16 the night before; tag each bank story with the 2–3 LPs it evidences. They will ask "tell me about a time you disagreed and committed" using those words.
  • Meta-style loops weight impact and speed: emphasize the metric beat and the shipped-fast beat of your stories. Google-style weight collaboration and general cognitive ability: emphasize the how-you-thought beat.
  • Startups and scale-ups probe range and self-direction: your side project is the trump card — you've been PM, engineer, and support desk for the same product. Big companies probe depth and process instead: lean on the release discipline and quality-gate stories.
  • Research ritual per company, 30 minutes: the product (use it), the engineering blog (skim 3 posts), the values page (note the exact words), recent news (funding, launches, layoffs). Then re-tag your ten stories against their vocabulary.
  • US norms worth knowing: interviewers are trained to probe with follow-ups, not new questions — expect "why?", "what would you do differently?", "what did your manager think?" three layers deep. That's standard, not skepticism.

day 2 · 10:00 · 90 minthe decision-maker

The hiring manager round

The HM isn't grading your STAR form — they're deciding whether to stake their own reputation on you. Different stakes, different preparation.

YOU ALREADY KNOW THIS

You've been on the other side of this table: when you interviewed candidates for your own team you weren't scoring answers, you were asking "do I want this person in my standup, and can I give them the hairy project?" The HM round is that, pointed at you.

// the idea

  • The HM is answering four private questions: can you do the job, will you need managing, will you make the team better, will you stay. Every answer you give should quietly feed one of the four.
  • "Why us / why this role" needs a specific, checkable answer — their product, their scale problem, something from their eng blog. Generic ambition ("growth opportunities") reads as "any company would do".
  • "Why are you leaving?" — one sentence, true, forward-looking: "I've built the team and the quality bar; the next problems I want are at a scale my current company doesn't have." Never badmouth; the HM mentally replaces your current employer with themselves.
  • IC vs lead ambiguity — at 7 years with lead experience you'll be asked "do you want to manage or build?" Have a genuine answer with a throughline (e.g., "senior IC who leads through technical direction and mentoring — I lead because it ships better software, not to climb the management track"). Wobbling here kills offers.
  • Logistics questions — visa sponsorship, timezone overlap, relocation, notice period — get crisp, factual, unapologetic answers. Know your exact status and dates before the call; hedging on logistics spooks HMs more than the facts ever do.
  • The HM round is a two-way conversation — interrupting the script with a smart question about their roadmap mid-round is a feature, not a risk.
hm-answers.md
"Why this company?"
  weak    "I've always admired the brand and want to grow."
  strong  "Two reasons. Your fleet product runs into the exact
           problem I've spent two years on — multi-tenant
           quality at enterprise scale — and your eng blog's
           post on release trains matches how I already run my
           team. I'd be productive in week one."

"Manage or build?"
  strong  "Lead-flavored senior IC. I've led a team of five for
           two years and I like owning direction, reviews, and
           mentoring — but my leverage comes from being in the
           code. I'm not chasing a people-management title; I'm
           chasing harder problems with a team around them."

"Where do you see yourself in 5 years?"
  strong  "Owning a problem space, not just a codebase —
           staff-level scope. Whether that has 'manager' in the
           title matters less to me than the scope itself."

day 2 · 11:30 · 60 minproduct companies' favorite

Cross-functional & stakeholder questions

Product companies live and die on eng–PM–design collaboration, so they interview for it explicitly. Your job: sound like an engineer PMs fight to work with.

YOU ALREADY KNOW THIS

You've owned sprint planning and stakeholder management, and on your own product you are the PM. You know both sides of "can we just add one small thing?" — that's the entire competency they're interviewing for.

// the idea

  • The competency has a name: productive pushback. Not "engineers estimate, PMs decide", not "I do what product says" — a peer who engages with the why behind requests and negotiates scope in outcome language.
  • "PM asks for something you think is wrong": first understand the goal behind the ask — half the time there's a cheaper path to the same outcome, and proposing it is the best possible answer to this question.
  • "Non-technical stakeholder and a technical constraint": show you translate — "I don't say the API is rate-limited; I say the vendor lets us check 100 prices a minute, so a full refresh takes an hour — here are the two ways around it and what each costs."
  • "Deadline is impossible": never a flat no — options with prices: date with less scope, scope with a later date, both with more risk spelled out. You bring the menu; they pick.
  • Have one story where product context changed YOUR technical call — it proves you listen in both directions, which is rarer than pushing back.
pushback-script.md
PM: "Can we add real-time collaborative editing this sprint?"

reflex     "No, that's months of work."        # true, useless

productive "What's driving it — is this the enterprise deal?
            [listens] OK. Real-time co-editing is a quarter of
            work — CRDTs, presence, conflict UI. But if the
            deal needs 'two people don't overwrite each other',
            I can ship optimistic locking with a 'Priya is
            editing' banner in a week. Covers the demo, buys us
            time to decide if we ever need the full thing."

Goal understood → cheaper path → priced options → their call.

The mock circuit

Twelve questions, out loud, on a timer, in interview order. This is where written stories become spoken ones — the gap between the two is bigger than you think.

// the circuit — 45s think-cap, 2min answer-cap, recorded

  • Round 1 — the openers (15 min): tell me about yourself · walk me through your resume · why are you leaving · why us.
  • Round 2 — the core eight (45 min): a project you're proud of · a conflict with a colleague · a failure · an impossible deadline · a time you mentored someone · a disagreement with your manager · a time you influenced without authority · your biggest technical mistake.
  • Round 3 — the curveballs (20 min): what would your team say is your weakness · a time you changed your mind under new data · the accomplishment you're most proud of and why that one · what do you do when you disagree with a decision that's final.

  • If you can recruit a human (friend, partner, former colleague) for round 2, do — a face changes everything. Otherwise the recorder is honest enough.

Questions you ask

"Any questions for me?" is scored. Good questions signal seniority harder than most answers — and they're also how you dodge a bad team before it's your problem.

YOU ALREADY KNOW THIS

It's due diligence on a codebase you're about to inherit. You wouldn't join a repo without reading the CI setup, the open issues, and the last few postmortems. These questions are exactly that, asked politely of humans.

// the idea

  • Match the question to the audience. Peer engineer: "walk me through your deploy — commit to production" (the answer tells you everything about eng maturity). Manager: "how do you decide what the team works on?" and "when did someone on the team last get promoted, and what did that take?". Skip-level: "what does this team need to look like in a year?"
  • The two highest-signal questions anywhere: "what does success look like for this role in 6 months?" (vague answer = vague role) and "what's the hardest thing about working here?" (watch whether they're honest).
  • For senior roles, ask about scope and level explicitly: "what's the gap between senior and staff here, and where does this role sit?" It shows you think in leveling terms — and protects you from a down-leveled offer.
  • Red flags to listen for: nobody can name the last promotion · "we're like a family" · deploys are scary events · every answer about process is "we're figuring it out" (fine at 10 people, alarming at 500).
  • Never ask things the website answers, and keep comp for the recruiter — the loop is for the work, the offer call is for the money.

Offers & negotiation

The last conversation of the loop routinely moves compensation 10–20%. It's also the one nobody rehearses. One hour now is worth more per minute than anything else this weekend.

YOU ALREADY KNOW THIS

It's a vendor contract negotiation, and you've watched those: nobody accepts the first quote, line items move independently, and the side with alternatives wins. You are the vendor. Your alternatives are other loops — which is why you run several at once.

// the idea

  • US offer anatomy: base + equity (RSUs at public cos, options at startups, usually 4-year vest with a 1-year cliff) + bonus + sign-on. Total comp, not base, is the number that matters — and equity is often the most negotiable line.
  • Never give the first number. "What are your expectations?" → "I'm focused on fit; I trust you to make a competitive offer for the level — what's the range for this role?" US companies increasingly must publish ranges; ask for theirs.
  • Calibrate on levels.fyi for the company, level, and location (remote-from-India offers for US companies have their own bands — research yours specifically). Walking in blind is how down-leveled offers get accepted.
  • Level is the biggest lever. A senior-vs-mid decision moves comp more than any negotiation — if the offer comes in a level low, that's the thing to challenge, with your leveling evidence from day 1, before haggling dollars.
  • Negotiate once, specifically, warmly: "I'm excited, I want to sign. The equity is below what I'm seeing for this level — if you can get the grant to X, I'll sign this week." Competing offers are leverage stated as facts, never threats.
  • Everything is negotiable-ish: sign-on (easiest), equity, start date, title/level. Base moves least at big cos. Get the final offer in writing before resigning anything.

// reference · the meta-game

The behavioral playbook

The moves that apply to every question, every company, every round.

01 · pause before answering

Three seconds of "good question — let me pick the right example" reads as thoughtful, and buys your index lookup. Blurting reads as rehearsed or panicked; the pause is a senior move.

02 · answer the question asked

If they ask about conflict, don't tell your proudest-project story with a conflict garnish. Interviewers need evidence for their specific rubric box — off-target answers score zero no matter how good.

03 · it's fine to ask for a beat

"Can I take ten seconds to think of the best example?" is completely acceptable, twice per interview. So is "can I swap to a better example?" mid-answer — recovery reads better than commitment to a bad story.

04 · follow-ups are the interview

The prepared story opens the door; the three "why?"s behind it are the actual evaluation. You can't rehearse those — which is exactly why they must be TRUE stories about decisions you really made.

05 · one hour before every loop

Re-read the story bank (10 min), re-read their values page (5 min), re-record the pitch once (5 min), then stop. Over-rehearsing the morning-of makes you sound like a press release.

06 · after every real interview

Five minutes: which questions came up? Which story did you reach for and lack? Add it to the bank. Your bank compounds across loops — by loop three you're unshakeable.

// reference · scope control

Safe to skip (this weekend)

Real topics, honestly deprioritized. Know the one-liner; spend the hours on the story bank instead.

  • Brain teasers & estimation puzzles ("how many golf balls fit in a bus") — effectively extinct at product companies. If one appears, narrate assumptions calmly; the answer never mattered.
  • Company-specific trivia — nobody expects you to recite their org chart. The 30-minute research ritual is the whole requirement.
  • Hypothetical "what would you do" scenarios — they get graded with the same rubric as real stories, and your real stories are stronger. Bridge: "here's what I actually did in a similar situation…"
  • Personality-test prep (Hogan, Caliper, etc.) — some companies use them; they're explicitly designed to resist prep. Answer honestly and spend the evening on your pitch instead.
  • Memorizing 100 questions — the bank above covers the space. Ten deep stories beat a hundred shallow answers; every question is a routing problem, not a recall problem.
  • Salary-negotiation game theory rabbit holes — the scripts in offers cover 95% of real situations. The remaining 5% is "get a competing offer", which is a pipeline problem, not a script problem.

Writing is not rehearsing. Record yourself, listen back, redo. Do the two days, then one voice-memo story per day until the onsite.
